# EvoAudit / Beneath the Surface

## What it is

EvoAudit is a diagnostic-and-redesign study of **agent harness evolution** — recursive self-improvement in which model weights stay fixed while the surrounding harness (prompts, skills, tool interfaces, orchestration, sometimes source code) is persistently rewritten. The primary source is the arXiv preprint "Beneath the Surface: What We Overlook When We Talk About Agent Evolution" (arXiv:2611.07624, submitted 5 November 2026; cs.AI; released under CC BY 4.0; preprint, no peer-reviewed venue is claimed). The paper lists sixteen authors — Xiaobo Wang, Zimu Wang, Siyu Ye, Jiaqi Li, Min Tang, Jun Bai, Yuling Shi, Ruobing Wang, Jingjing Zhang, Chaofan Wang, Kai Wu, Mengnan Qi, Kai Cai, Xiaodong Gu, Qi Liu and Zilong Zheng — affiliated with the University of Science and Technology of China, the University of California, Berkeley, Simple Agent Lab, the Beijing Institute for General Artificial Intelligence, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University. The paper links its code base at https://github.com/simple-agent-lab/RSIHub.

The paper's thesis is that a higher overall benchmark score does not mean a more useful agent, because the score averages over tasks and trials: "newly solved tasks can conceal lost ones, while deployment cost and performance beyond the tasks that guided evolution remain unseen." It frames the problem as *feedback can be narrower than a change's effects* — a prompt, skill or execution routine can help the tasks that supplied feedback while disrupting behaviour elsewhere.

Three research questions organise the work: RQ1 (phenomenology) does evolution yield reliable and efficient improvement or suffer task-coverage trade-offs and hidden costs; RQ2 (generalization) do gains survive deployment on novel tasks; RQ3 (mechanisms) how do the individual evolutionary operators contribute to generating and preserving improvements. EvoAudit itself is the constructive follow-up: an agent-assisted workflow that runs diagnostics on archived evolution runs and returns revisions of four operators — **select**, **analyze**, **mutate**, **gate** — with evidence and validation records. Installing those four revisions into the A-Evolve recipe produces **AGE** (Audit-Guided Evolution).

## Mechanism

**The evolution process under study.** An agent A = (M, H) consists of a frozen model M and a harness H. An evolution algorithm E starts from a Seed A₀ = (M, H₀) and runs for G generations; at each generation a Parent is selected, C_g is the set of proposed harnesses produced by E(H_<g, Z_<g, D_evo; B_evo), and a rule Q selects the final Champion H⋆ from {H₀} ∪ ⋃ C_g. Z_<g is the accumulated evaluation evidence, D_evo the task set available during evolution, and B_evo the search budget. This framing follows AHE and GEPA.

**Benchmarks, harnesses, methods.** General tasks are τ³-Banking with 97 tasks; coding tasks are Terminal-Bench 2 with 89 tasks. Both are split into Train/Validation/Test (Train 50 tasks, Validation 20 on τ³-Banking and 19 on Terminal-Bench 2, Test 27 and 20), and evolution uses Train and Validation evidence while Test is reserved for Seed and Champion evaluation. Two harnesses are used: MiniSWE 2.4.5, which is *source-editable* and permits inner-source changes, and Codex 0.143.0, which is *outer-layer-only* and permits changes to prompts, skills, configuration and external orchestration. Four recipes are evaluated with GPT-5.4: AHE and HyperAgents are *implementation-centric*, A-Evolve and GEPA are *reflection-centric*. Each recipe specifies how seven operators are implemented — select, rollout, analyze, mutate, validate (optional), gate, record — and the mechanism analysis focuses on the four that determine which Parents are used, how evidence guides changes, and which Candidates persist.

**Metrics.** *Reliability* Avg@k = (1/Nk)ΣΣ Y_ni(A) averages per-pass success; *Coverage* Pass@k = (1/N)Σ 1[max_i Y_ni = 1] counts tasks solved at least once in k passes; *Std@k* is the population standard deviation of per-pass success rates; *task turnover* counts coverage gains and losses relative to the Seed. Cost is accounted on two sides: C_evo sums the cost of evolution calls, and the estimated per-task deployment cost C_dep@k = k · c̄_dep^(k), where c̄_dep^(k) is the mean recorded cost per task execution over the first k passes.

**What EvoAudit diagnoses.** The *phenomenology* analysis compares each frozen Champion with its matching Seed over eight passes. The *generalization* analysis separates Train/Validation/Test contributions from external transfer — general-task transfer from τ³-Banking to the full τ²-bench (278 tasks across retail, airline and telecom, 22,240 verified evaluations) and coding-task transfer from Terminal-Bench 2 to DeepSWE v1.1 (113 official tasks, eight passes, 9,040 complete logical outcomes). The *mechanisms* analysis runs four controlled interventions: a common-process Parent expansion to test improvement potential, component interventions to test change compatibility, and frozen-pool admission replays to test retention.

**AGE's four operator revisions** are the concrete engineering content of the paper.

- *Select: regression-aware tie-breaking.* The highest-score objective is preserved and only exact ties are revised. Among the tied set T = argmax_C s(C), the rule minimizes the Seed-relative regression count R(C) = Σ_{t∈D_val} 1[r̄_t(C) < r̄_t(A₀)], sampling uniformly from the argmin. Comparable task-level evidence requires a unique eligible Seed evaluation, matching task-set hashes and task identities, equal per-task repetition counts and completed trials with finite rewards; otherwise the seeded generator samples uniformly from the original tie set.
- *Analyze: behaviour-grounded diagnosis.* The analyzer collects the current rollout and, when available, one preceding rollout along the selected lineage, keeping the last 30 records in stored order. Each judge input contains chronological tool calls, successful and failed tool results, and the completion signal; tool arguments are capped at 600 characters and results at 800, and each rendered trajectory uses at most 12,000 characters, preserving roughly two thirds from the beginning and one third from the end when truncated. The behaviour bundle **excludes benchmark rewards, verifier outputs, hidden tests, raw task text and raw-case paths**. The judge returns a score, category, outcome and failure_reason, where 0–3 means visible breakdown, 4–6 partial or ambiguous progress and 7–10 visible completion supported by an appropriate check; verdicts separate *Observed:* behaviour from *Inference:* explanation, and completion judgements must be grounded in trajectory evidence. Each request permits one attempt with a 600-second timeout; invalid or failed responses return an unknown verdict with sentinel score −1. The selected text view is capped at 30,000 characters for τ³-Banking and 60,000 for Terminal-Bench 2, and mutation consumes at most a 50,000-character prefix; both truncation steps add explicit omission markers.
- *Mutate: mechanism-linked, dependency-closed changes.* The mutation agent receives the selected behaviour evidence, the Parent workspace, the prompt and skill inventory and an editing contract. Before choosing files its instructions require the observed behaviour, the inferred mechanism, the replacement behaviour, the activation conditions and the expected trade-off; a recurring concrete pattern or a single severe, visibly supported breakdown can motivate a change, while ambiguous evidence favours preserving current behaviour. It must inspect affected interfaces and their producers and consumers, make one coherent intervention, preserve useful behaviour in successful traces and check for conflicting or duplicated guidance.
- *Gate: score-first, cost-aware admission.* Admission requires accept(C, P) = 1[s(C) > s(P) ∨ (s(C) = s(P) ∧ Q(C, P) ∧ c(C) < c(P))], where c is the recorded USD cost of canonical Validation evaluation and the comparability predicate Q requires matching task-set hashes, finite strictly positive costs and a direct Parent evaluation. When cost comparability fails, only the score-improvement branch applies.

**Run configuration for AGE.** Twenty generations, one proposed Candidate per generation, search random seed 0. Every rollout executes the 50 Train tasks afresh; canonical scoring executes each Validation task once (20 tasks on τ³-Banking, 19 on Terminal-Bench 2). Diagnosis and mutation use Codex 0.146.0 with GPT-5.4 at xhigh effort through Harbor; the target harnesses use GPT-5.4 at high reasoning effort. The optional *validate* stage is disabled, and the reference *rollout* and *record* implementations are unchanged.

**Repository evidence.** The inspected RSIHub repository is a Python framework with a `library/` of operator implementations (`library/analyze/` containing `ahe.py`, `artifact_rubric.py`, `failure_patterns.py`, `gepa.py`, `trace_browser.py`, `trajectory_only.py`; `library/gate/` containing `ahe_artifact_valid.py`, `hillclimb.py`, `parent_eligible.py`; a `library/_methods_shared/gepa/` directory; and `library/_shared/harbor/`), plus `src/evolve/` integration code, `containers/` definitions for candidate, mutate and mutate-codex workers, per-operator documentation under `docs/reference/operators/` (analyze, gate, mutate, novelty, record, reflect, rollout, select, validate), and `tests/` files covering agent driving, evidence, handoff, isolation, launcher, observability, orchestration, queue, runner and research. The README describes the composition model in terms that match the paper's framework: a recipe-driven `evolve.yaml` names the target seed, the mutable surface, one operator per stage, the evaluator and the train/gate/sealed split; the loop is **select → rollout → analyze → mutate → guardrail → evaluate → absorb**; there are nine named stages (five required — select, rollout, mutate, gate, record — and four optional — analyze, validate, novelty, reflect); an *operator* is an implementation of a stage at `library/<stage>/<name>.py` and a *recipe* is a code-free selection and configuration of operators; and "Evaluation is never a selectable operator — it stays framework-owned", with every trial executed on Harbor. The README also states that each recipe declares its mutable paths and that "the evaluator, the archive stamps, and the framework code itself stay outside that surface". The repository also publishes per-task transition assets under `docs/assets/benchmark-deltas/` (files named for gain counts, loss counts, gain percentages and no-change counts), consistent with the paper's emphasis on task-level rather than aggregate accounting. In the 753-file tree inspected, **no file or directory is named for EvoAudit or AGE**; the four revisions are described in the paper as installed into the A-Evolve recipe.

## Limitations

**Stated by the authors.** The paper's ethics statement is explicit: experiments run in isolated benchmark environments rather than live user-facing services; evolving agent instructions and code can introduce regressions and unintended tool use; fixed evaluation contracts and harness-specific editing permissions are retained, and regressions and transfer are examined alongside aggregate performance; and "neither benchmark gains nor expert endorsement establishes safety for deployment". The discussion concedes that evidence supporting one decision need not support the next — that current performance, improvement potential, change compatibility and retention are distinct objectives — and that a conservative gate can discard stronger versions. The behavioural audit is explicitly an agent-assisted audit of 22 selected task–lineage comparisons (the largest positive and negative eight-pass success-count changes within each lineage, with ties broken lexicographically by task identifier), and the human evaluation is a separate, smaller exercise.

**Assessed from the reporting and the repository.**

- **Single source, no released result artifacts.** Everything rests on one arXiv preprint (v1, 5 November 2026). The paper links RSIHub, but the inspected tree contains neither the paper's tables nor a script that regenerates them, so the sixteen-lineage comparison, the transfer matrices and the admission-replay scores cannot be recomputed from the citation. No independent replication is known.
- **Small task sets, small turnover counts, and single-seed search.** The benchmark task sets are 97 and 89 tasks, and the headline phenomenon — "all sixteen Champions gain and lose covered tasks" — includes cells as small as one gained task and two lost ones. That the pattern is universal across sixteen lineages is the strong form of the finding; any individual row in Table 10 is a small-sample observation, and a single flipped task moves Pass@8 by roughly one point on Terminal-Bench 2 and one point on τ³-Banking. The five most consequential cells are also single-seed: the AGE runs use 20 generations with one proposed Candidate per generation and search random seed 0, there is no seed sweep over the evolutionary search, no confidence intervals on any reported metric, and no significance testing in the inspected text. A stochastic search that proposes one candidate per generation is exactly the kind of procedure whose outcome distribution needs replication across seeds.
- **Baselines are author-run.** AHE, HyperAgents, A-Evolve and GEPA were reimplemented by the authors inside RSIHub under one common process, and Appendix A documents how each recipe's seven operators were realised. Appendix A.3 argues this is necessary for comparability — "similar final gains can therefore arise through different processes" — but it also means the compared methods are the authors' readings of others' papers, and the fixed model (GPT-5.4), harness versions and budgets are 2026-era hosted settings.
- **AGE is installed into one recipe.** The four revisions are evaluated inside A-Evolve, so the paper's own claim is that AGE improves over its *reference method*. The cross-row reading noted above shows AGE also has the highest Avg@8 in each of the four settings of Table 10, but that is an observation from a single seed of a single run configuration, not a tested claim.
- **The "5.2 points but ten lost tasks" case is the honest headline.** The τ³-Banking/MiniSWE/HyperAgents lineage gains +5.2 reliability points while losing ten previously covered tasks and gaining thirteen. Any deployment decision that reads only the reliability delta would adopt that agent.
- **The transfer result is target-specific and partly paradoxical.** Coverage contracts on the general-task target and expands on the coding target, while reliability moves the other way: all four source-editable Champions improve general-task transfer reliability and lose coding-task transfer reliability. The paper reports this as "transfer selectively preserves gains" without a mechanism beyond feedback breadth, which is itself a limitation — the phenomenon is documented, not explained.
- **The behavioural audit is an LLM-assisted reading of traces.** Twenty-two comparisons, five behaviour categories, and the motivating contrasts (a Seed that loses a write-ahead log during inspection and a Champion that preserves a copy; a Champion that confuses "o" with "0") are informative but are a small, selected, non-blinded sample. The paper is careful to say these contrasts "motivate preservation and end-to-end checks rather than equating every outcome flip with acquired or lost task knowledge".
- **Expert review is small and structured.** Eight experts, four revisions, and a protocol in which each expert chooses a direction before seeing the proposal. The scores cluster in the 6–6.75 range on an 8-point scale, which is endorsement with reservations; no inter-rater agreement statistic is reported for these four measures.

## Competing approaches

The paper compares four harness-evolution recipes directly, and the paper's own appendix describes each one's seven operator policies, which is what makes the comparison meaningful:

| Operator | AHE | HyperAgents | A-Evolve | GEPA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| select | Newest eligible Parent | Score-weighted sampling with a child-count penalty | Highest-scoring eligible Parent | Per-task Pareto-front sampling |
| rollout | Reuse Parent evaluation traces | Reuse Parent evaluation traces | Fresh Train trajectories | Fresh Train trajectories |
| analyze | Per-task debugger reports | Metrics and browsable raw traces | Behavior-only trajectory judge | Focused reflective examples |
| mutate | One testable target change | Edit the target and allowed operators | Distill experience into target changes | Reflection-guided target revision |
| validate | — | Python syntax checks | — | Strict improvement on the Parent's Train batch |
| gate | Admit valid completed evaluations | Admit valid completed evaluations | Strict improvement over the matched Parent | Admit valid completed evaluations |
| record | Outcomes and change evidence | Lineage experience | Outcomes and rationale | Reflection and batch experience |

Search scores use Train tasks for AHE and HyperAgents and Validation tasks for A-Evolve and GEPA; Test outcomes remain excluded from search. The paper's *select* result is that these native rules do not agree with improvement potential: below random on general tasks, above 55% on coding tasks.

**Cited as related work.** On agent optimization and evolution: Reflexion (episodic linguistic feedback), Self-Refine (iterative revision of the current output), ReflectEvo (small models trained on self-generated reflections), RAM (learning from communications to improve external memory), The AI Hippocampus (survey of memory mechanisms), DSPy (optimising language-model programs), TextGrad (propagating textual feedback through computational graphs), Promptbreeder (evolving task and mutation prompts), ACE (evolving context playbooks) and CoEvoSkills (co-evolving multi-file skill packages with a surrogate verifier). On harness and program evolution: GPTSwarm (optimising prompt-bearing graph nodes and orchestration edges), AgentSquare (searching combinations of planning, reasoning, tool-use and memory modules), ADAS (a meta agent generating agent programs), STOP (revising optimizer scaffolding), the Darwin Gödel Machine (evolving its own coding agent) and AlphaEvolve (evolving target algorithm programs against executable evaluation feedback). RSIHub itself is cited as "a composable framework for agent-evolution methods" that provides shared evaluation and lineage tracking — the paper's own code base. On capability boundaries and mechanisms: studies distinguishing small-k from large-k coverage expansion, token-level analyses of learning signal at high-entropy decisions, on-policy distillation studies, and feedback-shaping work such as UAPO and SAVE. On evaluation of agent systems: "AI Agents That Matter" (cost, reproducibility and generalisation beyond aggregate success), Wang et al.'s rethinking of harness-evolution evaluation (inconsistent advantages over test-time scaling under comparable budgets, limited held-out generalisation), scaling-of-agent-systems work, and the τ-bench and Terminal-Bench 2 benchmarks themselves.

Within this knowledge base, EvoAudit is the reference treatment of [[concepts/preservation-aware-agent-improvement]] and the reference counterpart to [[projects/procgrep]]'s procedural view: EvoAudit measures what evolution *lost* at task level, ProcGrep measures what the agent *does*. Both are instances of [[concepts/verified-completion-vs-apparent-success]] — an aggregate metric reporting success is a claim about a score, not about the set of tasks. Analyst-level comparisons, not source-cited dependencies, also extend to [[projects/strategist]] and [[projects/envopt]].

## Implications

The following is analysis by the KB maintainers, not a claim of the authors.

The paper's most transferable contribution is a *reporting discipline*, not an algorithm. Its core finding — that all sixteen evolved agents both gained and lost covered tasks — is nearly trivial to state and easy to skip, and the reason to adopt it is that it changes what a team is allowed to claim. If gains and losses must be reported separately, then "our harness evolution improved the benchmark by 5.4 points" stops being an admissible sentence without the accompanying "and lost four previously covered tasks". That is a small change to a template with a large effect on what gets noticed, and it costs only that the team keep a per-task ledger — the same ledger the repository's `docs/assets/benchmark-deltas/` per-task gain/loss assets already reflect for its own results.

Second, the "evidence must match the decision" principle is the sharper idea. The paper separates four decisions that are habitually collapsed into one score: which Parent to expand (measured here by *subsequent* gain, not current score), how to diagnose (measured by whether the specific change beats a generic reminder), what to change (measured by compatibility with the rest of the candidate, not by the change in isolation), and what to retain (measured by retained quality *and* preserved alternatives, with set size visible). Each of these was measured with a different, decision-appropriate evidence source, and each produced a different answer. A team that reuses one aggregate score for all four is, in the paper's terms, evaluating the wrong thing for three of them.

Third, the cost result deserves more attention than the paper gives it. Eight of eleven reliability-improving Champions cost more per trial, and the reliable improvement is partly bought with tokens that are only visible in a cost column. The finding that higher per-trial cost can coexist with lower cost-to-coverage (MiniSWE with AHE reaching Seed coverage in four passes instead of eight) is the useful nuance, but it also means "cost per attempt" and "cost to reach a target coverage" must both be reported, and the second only exists if coverage is measured at multiple pass counts rather than one.

Fourth, AGE's *analyze* operator is a transferable design in its own right, independent of the paper's numbers. Bundling behavioural evidence *without* benchmark rewards, verifier outputs, hidden tests or raw task text, and separating `Observed:` from `Inference:` in the verdict, is a concrete way to stop a diagnosis loop from optimising the evaluator instead of the behaviour. The related choices — capping tool arguments at 600 characters and results at 800, truncating a rendered trajectory to 12,000 characters while preserving both ends, refusing to truncate silently — are the kind of detail that determines whether such a loop is auditable. The exclusions are the important part: the diagnosis must never see the answer it is trying to generalise to.

Fifth, the harness distinction is a real, underappreciated variable. The source-editable harness (MiniSWE) and the outer-layer-only harness (Codex) behave differently under the same recipe — implementation-centric gains persist to Test in seven of eight cases, reflection-centric gains improve in three of four source-editable settings and in none of the four outer-layer-only ones. For anyone planning a self-improving harness, this says the *kind* of thing you let the system rewrite is a first-class design decision, at least as consequential as which rewrite strategy you use.

Finally, a caution the paper supplies itself. Its own ethics statement concludes that neither benchmark gains nor expert endorsement establishes safety for deployment. The paper's finding that every evolved agent lost tasks, and that stricter admission can discard useful alternatives, is the strongest argument in the literature for keeping human or independent gates in the loop — which is precisely the [[concepts/verified-completion-vs-apparent-success]] discipline applied to self-improvement itself, and the reason [[experiments/hidden-acceptance-regression-ingestion]] is the appropriate ingestion shape for any of these lineages.

## Reproducible experiment

RSIHub exists and the paper specifies its protocols in unusual detail, so an independent check is feasible. No such check is executed here; this is a proposed protocol.

1. **Replay one lineage and check the turnover ledger.** Configure RSIHub with a single recipe (A-Evolve is the reference), one harness, and one benchmark split; run 20 generations with one candidate per generation; then evaluate Seed and Champion over eight passes on the same task set. The pass criterion is the paper's central claim: every Champion should both gain and lose covered tasks. A lineage with no losses — meaning the aggregate delta is pure expansion — would falsify the paper's headline as stated and would be a strong result either way.
2. **Compare the two admission rules on the same frozen pool.** Take the archived candidate pool from step 1 and replay admission under (a) the native rule and (b) the constrained rule requiring higher mean success and incumbent-relative mean regression ≤ 0.05, then compute size-normalized best-Candidate retention. The paper predicts the constraint reduces the number of pools retaining a best candidate from fifteen to six, and that the size-normalized score falls for a source-editable harness and rises for an outer-layer-only one. Both directions are cheap to check and jointly quite discriminating.
3. **Test the select rule against a random baseline.** Build four-Parent pools and measure how often the native selection rule picks the Parent with the largest subsequent mean improvement, against a 25% random baseline. The paper's split prediction — below random on general tasks, above 55% on coding tasks — is a specific, falsifiable shape; if your implementation reproduces a flat curve near 25%, the domain-dependence is the interesting finding.
4. **Seed sensitivity.** Repeat the AGE configuration under at least three search seeds and report per-task gains and losses for each, plus the spread of Avg@8 and Pass@8. The paper reports one seed (0); the seed sensitivity of a one-candidate-per-generation search is unmeasured in the source, and this is the cheapest way to find out whether a 3.5–7.9-point improvement is stable.
5. **Cost accounting, both ways.** Report deployment cost per trial *and* the number of passes needed to reach the Seed's eight-pass coverage, and evolution cost separately, for every lineage. A result that reproduces the paper's pattern (higher per-trial cost, fewer passes to equal coverage) is more informative than a single cost delta, and it is the measurement a deployment decision actually needs.
