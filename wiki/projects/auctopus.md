# Auctopus

## What it is

Auctopus is a multi-agent AI laboratory built by El Mustapha Mansouri, Eikichi Kimura, Masato Koga and Keigo Arai (Institute of Science Tokyo) to carry out a complete experimental investigation. The work is reported in "Collaborative AI agents for autonomous experimental research with solid-state quantum spins" (arXiv:2610.40589, 29 October 2026). "Auctopus" is not the paper's title: it is the name of the MIT-licensed open-source release of the laboratory software, and this page uses it as the system's name. The repository is listed in this page's sources on the strength of the paper's release statement; it was not independently inspected for this page (see Evidence status). Experimental data and analysis code are available only from the corresponding author on request.

In a single investigation, the system generated and filtered its own research questions, proved a new exact result — a calibration law for ensembles of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centres in diamond — stress-tested that result computationally, designed an experiment to discriminate the law from interpolation methods, ran the measurement on a real quantum-spin instrument, rejected its own first experimental run as inconclusive when control measurements failed, revised the protocol without relaxing its acceptance criteria, and obtained support for the law in a second run.

Humans were not absent: they assembled the apparatus, described its capabilities, and configured a safety agent with preset operating limits, including maximum voltage and power. The autonomy claim holds within those human-set boundaries. The system's organising technique is cross-agent critical evaluation — generation and assessment are deliberately separated across agents holding distinct contexts, and predictions and acceptance criteria are committed to before results are judged — rather than instrument control alone.

## Mechanism

**Roles and orchestration.** Six principal agent roles — proposers, a principal investigator (PI), a computational researcher, an experimentalist, a manager, and independent reviewers — plus a safety agent whose approval was required before experimentation and a separate feasibility reviewer for experimental designs. Agents exchange written documents (proposals, manuscripts, research reports) relayed by the manager; assessments at decision points end with an explicit verdict and written reasons, and revision requests specify numbered changes. To reduce self-confirmation bias, fresh proposers never saw one another's proposals, each proposal batch was assessed in a new PI conversation, and the final manuscript reviewers started in new conversations and received only the manuscript — no exchanges, source code, or raw data. Raw photon counts stayed on the instrument computer; the PI and computational researcher judged measurements from the experimentalist's laboratory reports. Model assignments are author-reported: the PI ran GPT-6 at the "Pro" reasoning setting, proposal generation GPT-5.6, the computational researcher GPT-6 Astra at maximum reasoning effort through Codex on the TSUBAME 4.0 supercomputer, the experimentalist Claude Fable 5 through Claude Code on the instrument computer, and the manager Claude Opus 5 with browser and SSH access to the other machines.

**Question funnel.** Proposers were briefed to formulate a theoretical contribution to information science or machine learning in which physics is essential, applicable to NV centres, with at least one central prediction testable on the platform. Fresh batches launched ten proposer conversations and were forwarded to the PI once five proposals had arrived; a deduplication stage grouped proposals by central mechanism and kept one representative per group. The PI assessed novelty, logical validity, significance, theoretical feasibility and experimental testability, and could reject an entire batch. After twelve consecutive assessments without a winner, the manager added exclusions derived from repeatedly rejected mechanisms; revision batches launched six proposers, each given a rejected proposal verbatim plus the PI's full assessment.

**The physics problem and the law.** Individual NV spins experience different detunings and drive strengths, so the measured signal averages over an unknown distribution of pulse errors. The pulses have fixed duration, amplitude and shape but independently variable phases, and each spin is assumed to retain the same error parameters throughout a sequence (persistent pulse errors). The accepted proposal had established a polynomial upper bound: 8L² + 6L + 1 independent response functions suffice for sequences of up to L pulses, without determining the exact number. The PI reformulated the problem as the dimension of the ensemble-response space and proved the exact result: **d_L = L² + 1 calibration measurements are necessary and sufficient** to predict the ensemble response to every phase-controlled sequence of at most L pulses, independently of the distribution of persistent spin errors, and including an unknown common fluorescence background and contrast. Sufficiency holds because the calibration responses span the response space, so each target response is a fixed linear combination of calibration responses with coefficients summing to 1; necessity holds because with fewer calibrations, distinct physically admissible ensembles exist that agree on every calibration measurement yet differ on unmeasured sequences, so no predictor can guarantee recovery. A practical consequence is that the whole sequence family becomes predictable **without reconstructing the unknown error distribution**. The paper also gives a theoretical noise analysis: with determinant-maximising design and least-squares estimation, worst-case mean-squared prediction error is at most M·d_L/(N − d_L + 1) for N ≥ d_L observations with Poisson means bounded by M.

**Computational stress test.** The computational researcher independently reconstructed the response-space structure, confirming the dimensions the law predicts, and ran a PI-specified stress test of the persistence assumption: hidden phase rotations about each spin's z axis inserted between pulses, in a fixed six-pulse test case, with candidate ensemble pairs sought numerically and the resulting bounds certified in exact rational arithmetic.

**Discriminating experiment design.** The objective was to test the law on withheld sequence responses in a design where the law and specified interpolation methods predict differently. The PI rejected a first design — a complete 3×3 grid of nine three-pulse calibrations — because the grid already determines the targets by Fourier interpolation, so agreement would not distinguish the law from ordinary interpolation. The accepted cross-length design uses ten calibrations (five three-pulse sequences plus five shorter ones, including the empty sequence), verified by exact rank calculation to span the L = 3 response space, leaving four three-pulse targets that the competing interpolation methods cannot determine. Target predictions are linear combinations of the ten calibration means via a 4×10 transfer matrix. The primary statistic is a target contrast S that cancels the common fluorescence background, compared against the calibration-side prediction P derived from the same formula family. Both protocol interpolation baselines — a nine-term Fourier interpolant fitted to the five three-pulse calibration means, and a six-term alternative incorporating the symmetry of the repeated pulse — predict S = 0 for any calibration record; the protocol additionally required a nine-parameter model of coherent ensemble evolution fitted to all ten calibration means. Before acquisition, the PI fixed the transfer matrix, the definition of P, accuracy tolerances, controls and outcome criteria, and the decision rule itself was evaluated on 20,000 simulated runs per case, including a case with a target phase misprogrammed by 60°.

## Evidence

All results below are **author-reported from a single study**; none has been independently replicated (see Limitations and Evidence status).

**The law (proved result).** The PI agent tightened the proposal's 8L² + 6L + 1 upper bound to the necessary-and-sufficient count L² + 1. For L = 3 the required number of calibration measurements falls from the proposed bound of 91 to 10. The computational researcher independently reconstructed the response-space structure and confirmed dimensions 26 and 37 for sequences of up to five and six pulses — exactly 5² + 1 and 6² + 1. This internal confirmation is a second agent of the same system, not external verification.

**Stress test.** The persistence-assumption stress test produced **252,620 exact certificates**; for the specified six-pulse test case, the certified prediction error stayed at the photon-noise level for hidden phase rotations of approximately 1.2° or less.

**Question funnel.** Over approximately 30 hours, the PI rejected 75 proposals, accepting none during the first 17 batches, on grounds including already known, logically invalid, not significant, and no feasible test. Idea families excluded after recurring rejections had accounted for 52 of the 56 earlier fresh proposals; after the exclusions, nine new proposals explored seven previously unrepresented directions.

**The rejected first run.** The first main acquisition — 36 alternating ten-minute sub-runs, with two controller programs holding complementary global-phase replicas — **met all ten precommitted accuracy criteria**, which assessed agreement for the four withheld targets and a contrast formed from those responses. However, the global-phase and reference-pulse controls failed, and the PI classified the run **inconclusive**. The experimentalist's diagnosis: the signal rose by 7.6% over the morning and showed a further 2.9% step at 11:48 a.m., coinciding with a laboratory member switching on the room lights; because the two programs sampled the drifting signal at different times, nominally equivalent global-phase replicas acquired systematic offsets. The PI kept the inconclusive verdict and specified a revised acquisition; the acceptance criteria were not relaxed. A first pilot with 30-second alternation blocks still failed the phase controls; a second pilot implementing phase switching within a single program passed.

**The revised run.** The final acquisition collected **21,600 valid one-second records** over the prescribed six hours (720 blocks of 30 seconds, randomised sequence ordering) and passed all ten accuracy criteria and all experimental controls. The measured target contrast was **S = (40.0 ± 0.4)×10⁻³** weighted counts per shot, against the law's locked prediction **P = (39.7 ± 1.3)×10⁻³**; the interpolation alternatives predicted exactly 0. The precommitted tolerances required S within 20% of P and each target residual below 30% of P, with controls rejected at tail probability 10⁻⁴ and a variance-validity requirement on the excess-variance factor. The apparatus operated at a detected rate of 2.8–3.1×10⁶ counts/s (imposed limit 3.4×10⁶), a field of 4.18 mT, and a transition near 2.7503 GHz.

## Limitations

- **Prediction-lock timing (Methods 7.6).** The main text frames the predictions as fixed before the target measurements were analysed, but Methods 7.6 states that for the final acquisition "the documented prediction lock occurred after data collection and before any target mean was computed." The safeguard is therefore analysis-order discipline within a single acquisition — the lock preceded any computation of target means, but not the existence of the data. That is weaker than pre-registration before any data was collected, and this page reports the precise version.
- **No independent replication.** Every quantitative claim is author-reported from one run of one system. The internal "independent" checks — the computational researcher's reconstruction, the feasibility reviewer, the manuscript reviewers — are other agents or fresh conversations of the same framework, and several roles share model families (GPT-6 PI; GPT-6 Astra computational researcher). They provide process separation, not external replication.
- **Weak decisive rivals.** Both interpolation baselines predict S = 0 by construction, so the experiment demonstrates that the law's cross-length information transfer is real, but the headline result does not quantify performance against strong alternative predictors. The protocol-required nine-parameter coherent-evolution model, fitted to all ten calibration means, receives no headline outcome in the paper's results narrative, so its comparative performance cannot be assessed from the main text.
- **Restrictive assumptions, small experimental case.** The law assumes persistent per-spin errors, pulses differing only in phase, and a two-level model; the stress test probes one departure class (hidden z-rotations) at one specified test case. The experimental test itself is a single investigation at L = 3 with four withheld targets on one room-temperature NV apparatus.
- **Self-set, self-graded criteria.** The acceptance tolerances and control thresholds were fixed by the PI agent before the final acquisition — genuine precommitment within the study, but not externally registered or independently justified. Process metrics are likewise self-graded: the 75 rejections and the novelty and significance judgements are the PI's own verdicts under author-set criteria, and the funnel's false-rejection rate is unknown.
- **Agent inferences reported as diagnoses.** The drift explanation for the first run's control failures — including the causal attribution of the 2.9% step to the room lights being switched on — is the experimentalist agent's inference. It is timing-corroborated, but not independently audited.
- **Partial reproducibility artefacts.** Data and analysis code are on-request only. The released Auctopus code omits the prompts used in this study (templates only), so the exact agent configuration that produced the results is not public. Roles ran on specific proprietary models and APIs as reported in the Methods; rerunning with other models is untested in the paper, and total compute and API cost for the full investigation are not reported, so efficiency comparisons with human teams would be speculation.

## Competing approaches

Within the experiment, the law's live competitors were the two interpolation baselines (nine-term Fourier interpolation, in both grid and minimum-norm-fit forms, and the six-term symmetry-aware variant), the nine-parameter physical model of coherent ensemble evolution, and the displaced default approach: reconstructing the unknown error distribution ν by full ensemble characterisation before predicting responses.

The paper positions itself against earlier autonomous-science systems: the robot scientists of King et al., which coupled hypothesis generation with testing without LLM judgement layers; autonomous materials and chemistry laboratories such as A-Lab and the mobile robotic chemist, which close experimental feedback loops for experiment selection; end-to-end AI research systems (Lu et al.; Co-Scientist; Ghareeb et al.); and quantum-domain agent systems, of which Isogawa et al.'s agentic AI for autonomous quantum sensing is the closest same-domain comparator named by the authors. Sakana's AI Scientist, as evaluated by Beel et al. (2025), is cited as the cautionary case — a system that "reported performance improvements that were based on flawed experiments" — and Auctopus's rejection machinery is framed as the answer to that failure mode. The separation of generation from evaluation also answers the critique (Huang et al., ICLR 2024) that large language models cannot reliably self-correct their own reasoning.

## Implications

The result with the widest reach is procedural rather than physical: an agent system that rejects its own apparently successful run — one that met every accuracy criterion — because independent control measurements failed, and that holds the verdict and the criteria fixed while it redesigns the acquisition. That is precisely the property whose absence the paper's cited AI Scientist evaluation exposed, and it bears on any verification regime for autonomous research: see [[techniques/verification-and-evidence]]. Physically, the L² + 1 law removes the need to reconstruct an unknown error distribution in order to predict an NV ensemble's response to phase-controlled sequences, within the law's stated assumptions, which is directly relevant to calibration cost in quantum sensing and control. For multi-agent system design, the study is evidence that role separation with fresh contexts and precommitted, asymmetric acceptance criteria (support, contradiction, or inconclusive, fixed in advance) can produce self-correction that single-model self-critique does not. All of these implications are bounded by the evidence: one investigation, one platform, one small experimental case, and no replication.

## Reproducible experiment

A replicator needs an ensemble NV apparatus comparable to the paper's (room-temperature confocal microscope; Element Six DNV-B14 diamond at ~4.5 ppm NV; pulses of 64 ns with 152-ns intervals in the final sequences), the accepted cross-length design — ten calibrations (five three-pulse sequences plus five shorter, phases in multiples of 60°), four withheld three-pulse targets, the transfer-matrix prediction, and the S-versus-P contrast test with the paper's tolerances (±20% on the contrast, residuals below 30% of P, controls at tail probability 10⁻⁴) — and the amended acquisition protocol, in particular within-program phase switching, which the drift episode showed to be load-bearing: alternating between two programs let slow signal drift masquerade as phase-replica disagreement. The proof itself can be rechecked from the paper's mathematics, and the computational stress test is specified (test case, design of 192 calibration sequences and 3,715 targets, exact-arithmetic certification) but was run on a supercomputer. What public artefacts do not currently support is an exact rerun of the agent investigation: the Auctopus release provides the laboratory implementation with prompt templates rather than the study's prompts, and the experimental data and analysis code are available only on request from the corresponding author. No part of the system was executed or tested for this page.
