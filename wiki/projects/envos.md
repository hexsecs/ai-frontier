# EnvOS

## What it is

EnvOS is a framework for building and certifying computer-use environments that change mid-task, together with an evidence-based auditing pipeline for the scores those environments produce. It is not primarily an agent benchmark, and reading it as one inverts the paper's centre of gravity. The authors (Ankush Kadu, Athira Gopal, Niharika Singh, Arpit Jain, and Aswanth Krishnan, all at QpiAI, Bangalore) state the contribution directly: "We introduce EnvOS, a framework for building and certifying environments that change mid-task. Before any agent runs, EnvOS checks that each change fires correctly, that the task remains solvable, and that the grader separates correct from incorrect behavior; after each run, separate code re-checks the verdict from archived evidence." The paper was accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 Workshops on Agentic AI Benchmarks and Applications for Enterprise Tasks (AABA4ET) and on Continual Learning in the Era of Foundation Models and Embodied Agents (CL4FMAgents), and follows a companion workshop paper by the same team on a certified pipeline for evaluating agents under state-changing phenomena.

The focal phenomenon is *stale authoritative information*: "the agent is first served a task-relevant value, the underlying application state changes on a later eligible request, and the agent subsequently makes a consequential decision." The agent evaluation that demonstrates the framework covers exactly two workflows in one synthetic workplace testbed — clinic appointment booking and insurance-claim approval — and the authors explicitly refuse to pool its results into a model ranking: "These results cannot be pooled into a controlled model ranking." What the experiments actually estimate is narrower and more interesting than agent skill at coping with change: in the principal comparisons the authoritative change happens in *every* condition, and the only manipulated variable is what the agent is shown at the review step. Showing the updated value worked (15/16); telling the agent to go and check, with a reminder and a source link, barely helped (2/16).

## Mechanism

EnvOS organises evaluation as a three-stage lifecycle: **construct** a candidate task and intervention, **certify** it against declared obligations, then **execute** admitted instances with independently auditable outcomes.

The unit of construction is the distinction between a *phenomenon* and a *perturbation*. A phenomenon is "an application-independent specification of a class of authoritative-state changes and the conditions under which an instance of that class is admissible." An executable perturbation implements one instance, written ρ=(δ,τ,c,g): δ is an operator on authoritative state, τ identifies the triggering event, c governs subsequent disclosure through permitted observation channels, and g ensures the intervention fires at most once. The state transition and its disclosure are deliberately separate operations — a model-visible notice is never treated as the environmental event itself. For the stale-information phenomenon the trigger is exposure-conditioned and one-shot: "The focal stale-information intervention requires a task-relevant value to have been served before a later, exposure-conditioned mutation supersedes it. The original serve arms the trigger; a subsequent eligible application request applies the update." A change that fires before the old value is served does not count as a stale-information episode. The harness separately records the server-side page serve and the frame actually delivered to the model; neither, the authors note, proves comprehension.

Certification runs before any model, on the frozen generated instances rather than just templates, across five check families: intervention validity (the intended transition, the trigger boundary, no unintended changes), observation consistency (permitted disclosures match authoritative state, with the delivery path verified), feasibility (a scripted reference policy completes the task), behavioural discrimination (deliberately deficient scripted policies fail in the expected way), and scorer robustness (known corruptions, near-misses, valid controls). "Each check returns pass, violation, or monitor error; an unassessable obligation does not count as a pass."

In the experiments, what varies between conditions is review-time information, not the change. In clinic booking: **OFF-A** shows nothing extra at confirmation; **CUE-A** shows "a verification reminder and a link to the original record, with the updated location withheld"; **LIVE-A** shows "the same reminder and link with the current location shown." In insurance claims: **OFF-A** adds no policy information; **ON-A** "presents the current policy at approval." Controls are **-I** (an irrelevant-record change at the matched trigger) and **L0** (no change). As the authors put it, "the experiments estimate *within-configuration review-information effects*, not the effect of making a static environment dynamic." The clinic task combines mail, calendar, and a booking portal to select and confirm a compatible appointment whose session location is superseded after being served. The claims task requires approving an amount consistent with the policy in force at approval time — approved = min(claimed − deductible, current limit) — and some instances cross a calculation threshold, so a policy lookup alone does not guarantee a correct approval. The paper flags an asymmetry itself: unlike the clinic instructions, the claims task explicitly refers to the policy in force at approval.

Execution uses "a synthetic workplace testbed with 14 containerized infrastructure services, including clinic and insurance applications and supporting mail, calendar, and database services." The policy receives screenshot and text observations "through a controlled semantic-browser interface, acts through a fixed schema, and is invoked statelessly with a short memo and recent-action history," with budgets of 48 actions (clinic) and 40 (claims). "This interface does not constitute unrestricted operating-system-level computer use."

Scoring is validity-first: validity is classified before outcome, and "only valid episodes are assessed against applicable task invariants using the authoritative application state at the agent's consequential commit," against five registered endpoint requirements where applicable (correct target state, single commit, no collateral changes, required post-commit continuation, final report consistent with committed state). Source revisits are process measures only; revisiting alone does not guarantee success, and success does not require revisiting. After each run, "separate implementations reconstruct supported validity and endpoint terms from the preserved evidence. Unresolved terms remain unresolved when the archive lacks sufficient information." The whole apparatus is preregistered and frozen before rollouts — task instances, conditions, contrasts, budgets, validity rules, stopping rules, statistical analysis, and interpretation templates, with a release manifest hashing 29 repository paths — and every quantitative statement in the paper is emitted by a claim ledger that re-derives it from a hashed artifact and fails on any missing or drifting input. Reported configurations are model–invoker pairs, not pure models: the Claude models read each frame via a file-read tool, while Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview received frames inline in stateless REST requests with no tools. Statistics use a matched-instance paired design with exact two-sided sign tests, Newcombe 95% intervals for paired differences, per-block Holm corrections, and a retrospective 12-test paper-wide Holm family as a sensitivity analysis; condition order was fixed and counterbalanced, not randomised, with one sampled rollout per condition.

## Evidence

All results below are author-reported, derived from the authors' own claim ledger over their own archived artifacts; no independent replication exists. The prospective blocks are small: 16 clinic instances and 10 claims instances, one rollout per condition.

**Clinic booking, Claude Opus 5 (Block BC, prospective and preregistered).** Opus 5 passed OFF-A 0/16, CUE-A 2/16, and LIVE-A 15/16. The primary LIVE–CUE contrast had 13 treatment-only successes, 0 control-only successes and 3 ties (net +0.81 [0.49, 0.92]; exact p=0.000244; block-local Holm p=0.000488), and the rejection survives the paper-wide Holm analysis over 12 claim-bearing tests (adjusted p=0.00293). The secondary CUE–OFF contrast — the reminder-plus-link condition against nothing — had 2 treatment-only successes and 14 ties (net +0.13 [−0.09, 0.36]; block-local Holm p=0.5): the cue, on its own, did not demonstrably help. The LIVE-I irrelevant-change controls passed 3/3. Trajectory evidence, descriptive rather than a success criterion, is pointed: "The source page was accessible under every condition. Only 1 of 16 verification-reminder trajectories followed the supplied link." The independent reconstruction agreed with the evaluator on 51 of 51 counted rollouts and validity re-derivation on 53 of 53 attempts, with a stated caveat: the independent scorer reconstructs appointment availability only, while calendar compatibility is not re-derived and rests on design conditions. A separately registered Sonnet 5 clinic block was incomplete at its registered cutoff (21 of 51 cells counted) and produced no inference.

**Insurance claims, 10 frozen instances (seeds 0–9), paired OFF-A/ON-A.** Claude Opus 5 succeeded 10/10 in both conditions; because every OFF-A instance already succeeded, the registered rescue-opportunity gate was not met and no panel-effect claim is made for Opus. Process counts show Opus requested the policy record after the change in all 10 OFF-A runs, versus 1/10 for Sonnet — descriptive counts, with the authors noting the instruction itself may encourage checking. Claude Sonnet 5 met the full approval endpoint on 1/10 without the panel and 9/10 with it (8 pairs favoured the panel, 0 the other arm, 2 neither; net +0.80 [0.37, 0.92]; p=0.00781; controls ON-I 3/3, L0 1/1). Sonnet applied the superseded limit in 9/10 episodes without additional policy information. Crucially, this contrast rejects under its registered block-local analysis but **not** under the paper-wide Holm analysis (adjusted p=0.0781 over 12 tests) — a family the authors state was finalised after the claims outcomes existed, making that adjustment retrospective. The independent scorer agreed on 24 of 24 counted rollouts and validity re-derivation on 25 of 25 attempts. Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, in a separately registered follow-up reusing the same 10 instances, failed OFF-A on 10/10 and reached 5/10 under ON-A (5 rescues, 0 harms, 5 ties; exact paired p=0.0625), which did not meet the registered effect criterion.

**Evaluator audits (archive challenges).** These test the grader, not agents. In the sealed challenge (28 cases, built by a separate model, run once), "the outcome scorer met 28/28 sealed expectations, validity re-derivation 24/28 and archived evaluator replay 10/28, unrepaired" — with the authors noting this is "not blind validation." In the rule-only challenge (56 cases designed from rule excerpts alone; 36 admitted, run once; 26 corruptions and 10 controls), the pipeline accepted all 10 controls and caught 24 of 26 corruptions, counting none as clean, against 19 caught by raw-log baselines. In the external challenge — the least author-controlled set, designed and generated by a Gemini CLI agent — the pipeline caught 14/20 claims and 11/19 clinic corruptions (25 of 39 overall, against 4 for simple log checks), but also "counted 4 and 8 as clean" per workflow (12 of 39 designated corruptions accepted as clean in total) and "rejected 3 of 16 controls"; the authors dispute 19 of that set's expectations. A post-hoc, author-built, explicitly non-blind baseline comparison found the pipeline and a simple raw-log checker close on narrow in-scope clinic checks but far apart on claims breadth. The audits also did real work: "Certification, re-derivation and audits surfaced 11 defects, which now serve as regression cases. For 7, a previously scored archive already carried a model verdict under the defective code."

**Supplementary exploratory corpus (Appendix S1).** Under a different interface (Opus 5 via Claude Code with shell and GUI tools), three environments hosted 32 variants and 93 rollouts. The certification suite there comprises 389 executable assertions across 19 suites — "a *bounded certifier*, not a proof that an admitted evaluation is globally sound." Four phenomenon families selected from observed failures performed poorly (Contested Resource 1/5, Ghost Commit 0/6, Mandate Drift 1/9, Cold-Start Inconsistency 1/3; pooled 3/23), against aggregate unperturbed controls of 11/12. The authors state plainly that no count from this appendix enters the registered paired tests, the sensitivity family, or the main challenge totals, and that the families' comparison is descriptive, not confirmatory.

**Supplement self-reproduction (Appendix Y).** The authors report running their packaged supplement offline — no network, world, or model — with the claims target passing all 85 of its checks, the prospective target all 174, and the packaged ledger re-deriving all 2,319 of its entries. This run was executed by the authors on their own package; it is reported here as paper-stated, not independently verified.

## Limitations

Author-acknowledged limitations come first; analyst-assessed ones follow, labelled as such.

**Author-acknowledged.** The prospective evaluation "covers two workflows in a synthetic workplace under a controlled semantic-browser interface, with fixed, counterbalanced instance orders and one sampled rollout per condition," and "do[es] not establish performance in unrestricted native computer use or naturally occurring change-time distributions." The principal contrasts "manipulate review-time information while retaining the same authoritative mutation in both paired conditions; they do not estimate the causal effect of environmental change itself." Small paired samples and potential order effects limit generalisation, and "non-rejection does not establish equivalence." The framework evaluates agents but does not train them: whether certified non-stationary environments improve RL-trained computer-use agents is untested, so the training use is a proposal, not a result. "Served pages and delivered frames do not establish agent comprehension." In clinic booking, "calendar compatibility is not independently reconstructed from the archived records, and certain provider-side inputs and historical endpoints are incomplete." The "independent" checkers carry a definitional caveat: "*independent* refers to separate code paths developed by the same team, not an independent research group or independent source data," so agreement "does not establish that their common archived evidence is free of errors." On the audits, "external archive challenges reveal residual false acceptance and control-rejection errors, with some expected verdicts disputed; the case sets are not population-level error-rate estimates" — 12 of 39 externally designated corruptions were counted clean and 3 of 16 controls rejected. All prospective inferential claims rest on Claude-model blocks; the only non-Anthropic block is a separately registered follow-up on reused instances, and the Sonnet clinic block is incomplete. The paper-wide Holm family "was finalized after the claims outcomes existed," so its adjustment of the claims tests "is retrospective." A preregistered screenshot-only interface crossover stopped at its engineering admission gate (viewport fit failed on 18 of 144 page checks; legibility failed on 4 of 404 values). Certification itself is conditional: admission rests on the declared checks, "not a proof that every future model trajectory will be correctly handled," and the audits found 11 real defects, 7 of which had already produced scored verdicts under defective code. The paper also reports a non-replication of the team's own earlier result, with the earlier evidence retained in the supplement provenance, and lists permanently missing evidence (provider-side decoding defaults, token usage, exact per-step observations) that cannot be reconstructed.

**Analyst-assessed.** The self-evaluation structure runs throughout: the same team built the environments, the certifier, the evaluator, the "independent" reconstruction, two of the three challenge sets, and the raw-log baseline; the challenges were built by LLMs and the pipeline itself with LLM coding tools. The one genuinely external artefact — the Gemini-built challenge — is also where the pipeline looks worst and where the authors dispute 19 expectations; readers should weight that asymmetry. The samples are tiny: every headline effect rests on 16 or 10 instances with one rollout per condition, and the claims effects are one or two instance flips away from changing. Model–invoker confounding is total (different providers, delivery channels, and tool access vary together), so no cross-model comparison is licensed even though the abstract's juxtaposition of model names invites one. The measured construct is narrower than the "world moves on" branding: what is estimated is the effect of review-step disclosure format given a change that always occurs; with no no-change paired arm in the principal contrasts, the paper cannot say what the change itself costs any model. The task instructions are asymmetric — claims explicitly tells the agent the policy in force at approval governs, clinic does not — so Opus 5's spontaneous re-checking in claims may be instruction-following rather than change-detection, a reading the abstract's "re-checks on its own" leans against harder than the paper's own caveat supports. The supplementary corpus's four difficult families were selected because they were observed failures (disclosed by the authors), so the pooled 3/23 describes chosen hard cases rather than estimating difficulty. Finally, EnvOS is never run head-to-head against other dynamic-environment benchmarks on shared tasks; its practical advantage over simpler harnesses rests on argument plus the post-hoc, author-built raw-log comparison.

## Competing approaches

The paper situates itself among dynamic-environment benchmarks: OSWorld 2.0 (realistic computer workflows with mid-task information updates), Gaia2 (asynchronous changes and temporal constraints), InterruptBench (user goal changes mid-execution), StressWeb (interface and action-semantic perturbations), and DynamicGUIBench and AgentHijack (dynamic interfaces, observation and control corruption). The closest neighbour is STT-Arena, which builds dynamic tool environments and validates injected conflicts, timing, and plan disruption; EnvOS claims its distinction in prior-serve anchoring, matched review-step disclosure conditions, and archive-level checks, and frames its setting as a benign time-of-check–time-of-use gap rather than a newly identified phenomenon. Adjacent work on stale-state reasoning includes STALE (recognition and use of invalidated memories) and EnvProbe (when an agent re-queries its environment). For task generation, CUA-Gym co-generates instructions, environment states, and verifiable reward functions for computer-use training; the paper treats it as complementary — CUA-Gym scales environments and rewards for training, whereas EnvOS certifies that a mid-episode change and its verifier behave as specified. On evaluation validity, OpenComputer (verifiable software worlds, state-based judging), TheAgentCompany (multi-service workflows), and published critiques of log-based evaluation are the reference points. Inside the paper itself, the comparator is a simple raw-log checker that rebuilds verdicts from raw state, access logs, and served pages: the full pipeline beats it on claims breadth and roughly ties it on narrow in-scope clinic checks. See also [[techniques/verification-and-evidence]].

## Implications

The paper's central implication is about benchmark machinery, not model rankings: before an agent score in a changing environment means anything, the intervention, the observation delivery, and the grader each have to be certified and then audited against archived evidence — and EnvOS's own audits show why, since they surfaced 11 defects, 7 of which had already produced scored verdicts. The second implication is a design lesson for agent interfaces and review steps, and it runs against the intuitive advice. A reminder to re-check, even with a direct source link, changed almost nothing (2/16 versus 0/16, and only 1 of 16 cued trajectories followed the link); presenting the current value at the decision point is what moved outcomes (15/16). Systems that want agents to act on fresh state should surface the fresh state, not exhort the agent to fetch it. Third, the auditing toolkit is portable independently of the framework: preregistration with hashed manifests, frozen interpretation templates, claim-ledger generation of every published number, sealed run-once challenge sets for the grader, and full reporting of false accepts and rejected controls alongside detections. Fourth, the authors propose certified non-stationary environments as training grounds for RL on computer-use agents; that direction is explicitly untested here.

## Reproducible experiment

No code or data URL appears anywhere in the paper, and the anonymous supplement was not inspected for this page — it is described here only as the paper states it. Per the paper, the supplement includes the raw text archives, search logs, results, independent scorer outputs, and validity re-derivations for every prospective block, plus the environment, task-module, certifier, scorer, harness, invoker, and test sources, the pre-registrations, certificates, block specifications, and release manifests, and "a reproduction script that needs no network, world or model." Appendix Y describes the packaged supplement as 24,399 files (94.6 MB zipped) and reports the authors' own offline run of it: the claims target passed all 85 of its checks, the prospective target all 174, and the packaged ledger's verification re-derived all 2,319 of its entries. A researcher with the supplement could therefore re-derive every published count without any model calls; a researcher without it can still replicate the design at small scale — build one workflow in a containerised testbed, implement one exposure-conditioned stale-value perturbation, freeze and preregister a matched set of instances with OFF/CUE/LIVE review-step conditions, certify the instance set with scripted reference and deficient policies, and score validity-first from committed state — and test the paper's sharpest prediction: that showing the updated value at review rescues most failures while a reminder-plus-link rescues almost none.

## Evidence status

Every quantitative result on this page is author-reported. All numbers derive from the authors' claim ledger over their own archived artifacts, and the one reproduction run described was executed by the authors on their own supplement package. No independent replication exists or is mentioned. The reconstruction checkers are separate code paths by the same team, by the authors' own definition of "independent," and the external archive challenge was generated by an LLM agent (a Gemini CLI agent), not an independent research team. The paper also discloses an internal non-replication of the team's own earlier result. Partially mitigating factors, all author-provided: preregistration with hashed release manifests, frozen interpretation templates, claim-ledger macro generation of every quantitative statement, sealed and run-once challenge designs, and full reporting of misses — false accepts, rejected controls, and disputed expectations — alongside detections. Neither the anonymous supplement, any source code, nor any dataset was obtained or inspected for this page; no location for them is given in the paper, so their contents and the authors' offline-run claims are paper-stated, not verified.
