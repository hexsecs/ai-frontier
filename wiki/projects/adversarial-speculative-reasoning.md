# Adversarial Speculative Reasoning

## What it is

Adversarial Speculative Reasoning (ASR) is an inference-time intervention on **speculative decoding** that uses draft-model rejection as a control channel for numerical precision. It is reported in the arXiv preprint "Adversarial Speculative Reasoning Improves Long Horizon Agents" (arXiv:2611.05345v1, submitted 3 November 2026, cs.AI) by Hongyin Luo and Wei Fang (equal contribution), Lijie Yang, Owen Stepan, Daniel Suit, Jeff Krapf, Dana Wensberg and Jack O'Brien, affiliated with Subconscious Systems Technologies, Inc., Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Princeton University and Northeastern University. The paper is a preprint; no peer-reviewed venue is claimed, and no code or data release URL appears in the inspected text.

The claim under attack is the standard framing of speculative decoding as a pure systems optimization: a small draft proposes a block of tokens, the large target scores the whole block in one forward pass, a rejection-sampling rule accepts a prefix, and because rejection sampling leaves the output distribution exactly equal to the target's, the whole mechanism is judged only by acceptance rate. The authors argue this view fails for long-horizon agents, because a *served* target is not the idealized fixed function the exactness guarantee assumes — it runs under low-precision weights, quantized KV caches and sparse attention, and its logits are noisy. Their finding: numerical noise grows within a verification block, so where a token sits in the block determines how precisely the target encodes it; and once that noise is committed to the KV cache it resurfaces far downstream. ASR exploits this by training the draft model to propose the framing the target should *reject* at consequential reasoning points, so that the target itself writes those tokens at the head of a fresh verification block — the most precise slot — while the output distribution is untouched. The reported effect is 0.06%–0.28% of the deployed system's parameters trained.

## Mechanism

**The precision asymmetry (Section 3).** Two frontier open-weight reasoning models are served as they are deployed in the agent experiments, on SGLang across four NVIDIA B200 GPUs. *GLM-5.3* runs NVFP4 weights, an FP8 key-value cache and DeepSeek sparse attention, accelerated by a DFlash2 block-diffusion draft whose verification block has 8 positions. *DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash* runs a DSPARK draft proposing 5 tokens per step, giving 6-position blocks. Slot 0 of a block holds the last committed token; slots s ≥ 1 hold draft tokens; the distribution computed at slot s is the one used to verify the draft token at slot s+1 or to resample in its place.

The error metric compares log-probabilities token by token between two forward passes that should agree, over the four most likely tokens under a reference pass plus the token actually emitted; |Δ log p| is measured in natural-log units (nats), where 1 nat is a factor of e ≈ 2.7 in probability. Two complementary measurements are used.

*Sliding-window verification* isolates the numerical noise of a single forward pass at each position of a verification block. The prefix is held fixed in memory and only the forward decode pass is repeated (R = 4 times) on the unchanged KV cache, so the independent prefill noise of separate requests is excluded. A reference trajectory is generated by greedy single-token decoding so the tokens under test are the target's own choices. Measurements cover 1631 positions on GLM-5.3 and 1294 on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, drawn from DeepSWE prompts with 6k–24k tokens of context.

*Following a verify-written context* measures the downstream effect. In each GLM-5.3 DeepSWE trajectory the assistant turn with the longest reasoning is the *source turn* (median 9k tokens, after a median 45k-token prefix). Its tokens are written into the KV cache by two paths — (A) through DFlash blockwise verification with every source token forced and accepted through its verify slot, or (B) through single-token decoding. The token sequence is identical on both paths; only the computation that wrote the KV entries differs. The cache is then reused to score the remainder of the real trajectory teacher-forced, up to 128k further tokens (a median of 95 subsequent assistant turns), recording every assistant token's log-probability; tool outputs stay in context but are not scored. Each path is built twice, and the paths are compared through their own build-to-build deviations |A − A′| versus |B − B′|, because |A − B| contains both paths' variance. The study ran on 96 distinct tasks with 6.38M scored assistant tokens per build.

**Rejection as a control channel (Section 4.1).** Speculative sampling accepts a draft token v ~ q with probability min(1, p(v)/q(v)) and on rejection samples a replacement from the residual r(v) ∝ max(0, p(v) − q(v)). For every q the committed token is distributed exactly as p, so the draft cannot change the output distribution. What the draft *does* control is where the block ends:

> Pr[reject] = 1 − Σ_v min(p(v), q(v)) = TV(p, q)

A rejection places the replacement token at slot 0 of the next block, whose KV entries and those of following tokens are computed from the most precise slot rather than the tail of the old one. A draft that disagrees with the target at decision points therefore routes those decisions through the target's most precise path, while routine text keeps an ordinary high-acceptance draft.

**Decision points and preferences (Section 4.2).** The point where the agent commits is located through the *committing sentence* — the sentence in the reasoning that confirms observations, states next steps and commits decisions. A judge labels each segment of reasoning and returns the committing sentence verbatim (at most 35 words), fixing the decision point in the token sequence; the judge is the target model itself, so no stronger external model is involved. Two preferences are defined and deployed:

- *Decision frame* (GLM-5.3). Agents resolve ambiguity in recurring modes: a **principle** frame derives the choice from the task specification or interface semantics; an **evidence** frame cites something observed in the repository or a previous test; a **likely** frame guesses which option hidden tests expect; a **risk** frame picks the conservative option; a **simpler** frame picks the option needing fewer code changes. The mode predicts correctness: on GLM-5.3, decisions made in a principle or evidence frame are correct about 70% of the time, against 36% for likely and risk frames. Principle and evidence are preferred; likely, risk and simpler are adversarial.
- *Run tests* (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash). This model writes terser reasoning and two thirds of its likely-framed sentences carry no lexical marker of the analysed frames; its failures concentrate in decisions taken without validation — signing off on a case, or trusting an unreliable observation, without running tests. The preference is over whether the agent runs a test before committing to a plan, upon receiving a failing or unexpected tool output, and right after reasoning raises a new scenario the code may not handle. A continuation that executes a test or script is *preferred*; one that only issues other commands is *adversarial*.

**Fork pairs and decision windows (Section 4.3).** Preference pairs are built as *fork pairs* whose two sides share every token up to the decision point, rather than from two independent trajectories that differ everywhere. For each decision point in a real trajectory the reasoning up to the start of the committing sentence is rendered exactly with the served chat template, and n continuations are resampled from the target at temperature 1 with top-p 0.95; the judge labels each continuation, which must state its own committing sentence in the newly sampled text. A preferred continuation is paired with an adversarial one from the same fork, so the shared prefix cancels in the objective. Forks reaching only one side are dropped, pair counts are capped per fork, per sample and per task, and **whole tasks are held out**, since a random split would leak near-duplicate forks between training and evaluation. On GLM-5.3, 29 tasks yield 1,668 forks with n = 16 continuations each (28,356 samples) and 2,843 pairs from 28 tasks, with 6 tasks (619 pairs) held out. On DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, the run-tests preference gives 2,203 forks with n = 12 continuations of up to 1,536 tokens (26,418 samples) and 6,391 pairs from 92 tasks, with 10% of tasks (719 pairs) held out and 5,100 pairs used for training.

The objective is direct preference optimization applied to a window around the decision. For decision framing, W(y) starts 32 tokens before the committing sentence and runs to its end with weight w_t = 1, followed by a 128-token tail over which w_t decays linearly from 1 to 0 — about 200 tokens in total. For the run-tests preference, which has no single committing sentence, W(y) is the first 512 tokens of the continuation with w_t = 1. The reward is the weighted mean log-ratio of the trained draft q_θ to the frozen original draft q_ref, and the loss is −log σ(β[r_θ(y_w) − r_θ(y_l)]) + α·NLL_θ(y_w) with β = 10.

**Training the drafts (Section 4.4).** The draft input is the target's hidden states, and the reward needs the draft's distribution at every token of a continuation, so hidden states are extracted once per sample from an inference instance hosting the target, and the draft is trained offline on those features. For *GLM-5.3-NVFP4 DFlash2*, the draft reads hidden states from six target layers (5, 19, 33, 47, 61, 75) projected into the draft; its transformer layers and norms are trained in bf16 — 2.15B parameters — while the feature projection, candidate selector and target embedding and LM head stay frozen. For *DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash DSPARK* (about 14.3B parameters, 13.6B of them FP4 routed experts, released with inference-only kernels), the reference implementation is ported to differentiable PyTorch operations matching the reference kernels to within 1e-7 per expert and 3–5% per stage output; the attention projections, norms, hyper-connections, attention sinks and bigram head are trained (449M parameters) while the routed experts and input projection stay frozen. FP8-stored weights are trained with quantization-aware training: the forward pass fake-quantizes in the deployed block format (32×32 blocks with power-of-two scales), the gradient passes straight through, and the exported draft is re-quantized to the same format so the deployed draft computes exactly the function that was evaluated.

**Size of the trainable set.** Both targets have about 750B parameters (GLM-5.3: 753B; DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: 749B excluding its draft). The trainable set is 2.15B parameters for GLM-5.3 and 449M for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash — 87% and 3.2% of the respective drafts (2.46B and 14.2B), and 0.28% and 0.06% of the deployed target-plus-draft systems.

## Evidence

All results are **author-reported** from this single preprint; none has been independently replicated.

**Setup.** Two targets are used throughout: GLM-5.3-nvfp4 with its DFlash2 draft, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash with its DSPARK draft, both with exact verification. Two agentic coding benchmarks are used. *DeepSWE* (Huang and Jiang, 2026) gives the agent a repository and a task description in a mini-swe-agent harness with shell access, graded by hidden tests; the paper reports the fraction of the **113 tasks** whose hidden tests all pass. *NL2Repo-Bench* (Ding et al., 2025) gives the agent a requirements document and an empty workspace and requires building a multi-module Python library from scratch; the **104 generated repositories** are graded against the upstream project's pytest suite and the mean test pass rate is reported. To avoid reward hacking, internet connection is prevented for the development workspace. Both benchmarks run for hundreds of turns per task with contexts growing past 200k tokens. For each target, two servers are compared that differ *only* in the draft — the pretrained draft (*base*, i.e. standard speculative decoding) versus the adversarial draft — with target weights, serving stack, exact verification, sampling parameters and agent harness held fixed; each configuration is tested **five times independently**.

**Measurement of context precision.** On GLM-5.3, slot 0 is exactly as noisy as single-token decoding (0.473 vs 0.478 nats); beyond slot 0 noise rises by +0.0195 nats per slot (95% CI +0.0174 to +0.0214, about 4% of the slot-0 level per slot), and the last slot is **34% noisier** than the first. Large deviations grow faster than the mean: shifts beyond 2 nats rise from 3.2% of tokens at slot 0 to 6.2% at slot 7, and beyond 3 nats from 1.1% to 2.1%. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash shows the same pattern with about half the absolute noise and a much thinner tail: +0.0094 nats per slot (CI +0.0078 to +0.0110), shifts beyond 1 nat rising from 3.1% to 4.5% and beyond 2 nats from 0.41% to 0.68%. The paper's stated mechanism: a token at slot s attends to KV entries of slots 0…s−1 computed in the same launch rather than read from cache, so it inherits the accumulated variation of all earlier slots; slot 0 reads only cached entries, which is why it matches decoding.

Because agents sample at temperature 1 with top-p 0.95 or 1.0, the paper computes the practical weight of these shifts: a 1–2 nat shift in the emitted token's log-probability changes whether that token lies inside the sampling nucleus in **12%** of cases, 2–3 nats in **47%**, and more than 3 nats in **over 84%**.

For the long-horizon effect, the ratio of extreme deviations (>4 nats) between the verify-written and decode-written paths is 0.98 in turns 11–30, 1.09 in turns 31–50 and 1.16 in turns 51–70 (95% CI [0.98, 1.36]), returning to 0.99 in turns 71–90 "since fixed teacher-forcing tokens correct the biases". An independent pair of builds continued to 64k tokens gave 1.20 ([1.03, 1.40]) beyond turn 30, while the builds reported in the paper give 1.07 ([0.91, 1.24]) on the same tokens. The authors read this as "a 10 to 20% excess of extreme deviations that sets in after about 30 turns", noting that the two independent comparisons agree on direction while the size of the excess varies by about as much as it varies between bins. The effect is confined to the extreme tail: for deviations up to 2 nats the two paths are indistinguishable at every distance pooled over turns 11–90.

**Do the drafts learn the preference?** *Held-out preference accuracy* is the fraction of held-out pairs on which the draft's windowed reward is higher for the chosen side; an untrained draft scores 0.5 by construction. Each preference is trained in both directions. On GLM-5.3, adversarial training on decision frames achieves **0.635** held-out accuracy (seed-to-seed spread ±0.015); both directions give 0.59 and 0.635 for the decision frame and 0.83 and 0.85 for running tests. On DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash the adversarial run-tests preference reaches **0.85**. Two reference preferences are trained but *not* deployed, to mark what a draft cannot learn: how long the model thinks (0.54 forward, 0.65 adversarial) and whether the decision is right — for the latter the best of 36 forward and 12 adversarial configurations reach 0.61 and 0.57 on 92 and 128 pairs, inside the confidence interval of chance, with medians of 0.52 and 0.46. The authors' reading: a draft can pick up how a decision is phrased and what the model does next, but is not strong enough to learn semantic correctness — which is sufficient for the design, since the draft only needs to propose the framing the target rejects.

Deployed configurations: the GLM-5.3 DFlash draft is trained at β = 10, learning rate 5×10⁻⁶ and a 128-token decay window; the DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash adversarial run-tests draft uses QAT over the attention parameters, learning rate 2×10⁻⁵, α = 0.2 and a 512-token window, taken after the second epoch (held-out accuracy 0.70).

**Agentic coding performance** (five independent runs per configuration):

| Target / benchmark | Unbiased draft | Adversarial draft | Gain | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GLM-5.3-nvfp4 on DeepSWE | 60.6% | 67.6% | +7.0 points | 2.6 to 11.4 |
| DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on DeepSWE | 67.8% | 71.8% | +4.0 points | −1.3 to 7.3 |
| DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on NL2Repo-Bench | 65.0% | 66.4% | +1.4 points | 0.4 to 2.4 (p = 0.011) |

All three mean gains are positive and the two that reach significance are on different benchmarks with different targets and different drafts. The DeepSeek DeepSWE interval spans zero.

**Directional controls.** *Training the preference in the preferred direction hurts*: desirable drafts trained on the same fork pairs with chosen and rejected sides swapped land **below** the unbiased draft — GLM-5.3 drops from 60.6% to 55.7% and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash from 67.8% to 66.4%, against gains of 7.0 and 4.1 for the adversarial drafts trained on the same data. The forward drafts learn their preference at least as well (held-out accuracy 0.80 for the DeepSeek forward draft against 0.70 for the deployed adversarial one), so the preference direction is offered as the only factor of the gap.

*An unrelated preference changes nothing*: 27 DeepSWE runs of DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash served with drafts trained on three preferences the project did not deploy — whether the decision is right (15 runs), how long the model thinks (5) and the frame of the decision (7) — pool to **67.5%** against 67.8% for the unbiased draft (Welch p = 0.83), with no single group differing from the unbiased draft (p ≥ 0.16); the deployed run-tests draft is 4.3 points above the pooled unrelated drafts (p = 0.018). The decision-right drafts, which did not learn their preference, sit on the unbiased mean; the decision-frame drafts learned theirs (0.64 to 0.69 held-out) and still change nothing.

**Inference efficiency.** Acceptance is compared slot by slot between the adversarial and the untrained draft, teacher-forced on the target's hidden states over held-out continuations (GLM-5.3-nvfp4: 30 continuations of 512 tokens; DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: 240 continuations of 512 tokens, 160 at run-tests turns and 80 at decision points). On GLM-5.3 the seven slots move by +0.1, +0.1, −0.3, −0.4, +0.2, +0.1 and −0.1 points; on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash the five slots move by −0.4, −0.6, −1.0, −1.2 and −1.2 points. Both keep the shape of their acceptance curve. Under the greedy-chain approximation, the expected accepted draft tokens per block is **2.28 before and after** training on GLM-5.3, and 2.5 against 2.46 on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash — a 1.8% difference. The paper's summary: "we traded up to 2% decoding speed for significantly improved reasoning capability".

## Limitations

**Stated by the authors.** The paper has no separate Limitations section; the caveats it does state are these. The long-horizon excess is "confined to the extreme tail" and its size "varies between them by about as much as it varies between bins", so the measurement is read as a 10–20% excess rather than a precise quantity; for deviations up to 2 nats the two paths are indistinguishable. The excess ratio returns to 0.99 in turns 71–90 because fixed teacher-forcing tokens correct the biases. The claim that an agent "holds on more easily to decisions committed at higher precision" is stated as a **hypothesis** ("We hypothesize that over a long horizon..."). The authors acknowledge the gain is not from a better model — nothing in the output distribution changed, and the draft cannot tell a right decision from a wrong one. On the DeepSWE result for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, the 95% confidence interval spans zero.

**Assessed from the reporting.**

- **Single source, no artifact.** Everything rests on one arXiv preprint (v1, 3 November 2026). A full-text search finds no code or data release for the trained drafts, the fork-pair datasets or the measurement harnesses; the only repository URL is DeepSWE's own benchmark repository. No third party can rerun these configurations as written, and no independent replication is known.
- **The mechanism is a two-link chain, and only the first link is directly measured.** The measured link is numerical noise growing within a verification block (4% per slot, 34% noisier last slot) and appearing far downstream as a 10–20% excess of >4-nat deviations after ~30 turns. The second link — that higher-precision encoding causes the agent to *hold* better decisions — is asserted as a hypothesis and is never measured directly; the end-to-end task success is the only evidence for it, and it is a single link covering both a change in draft behaviour and a change in trajectory precision.
- **Effect sizes are modest and partly insignificant.** Two of the three headline gains have confidence intervals excluding zero (+7.0 with CI [2.6, 11.4] and +1.4 with CI [0.4, 2.4], p = 0.011); the third, DeepSeek on DeepSWE at +4.0 points, has CI [−1.3, 7.3] spanning zero. The +1.4-point NL2Repo-Bench gain is 1.4 points on a mean test pass rate.
- **Baseline-run variance is not separated from treatment effect.** Each configuration is run five times independently, but the paper does not report a variance estimate for the *unbiased* draft across runs on the same benchmark, so how much of the +4.0 on DeepSeek could be run-to-run noise is not stated.
- **The controls are strong but local.** The forward-direction control and the unrelated-preference pool are both convincing arguments that *this* preference and *this* direction matter. Neither excludes the possibility that any sufficiently different draft distribution would shift outcomes, nor that the effect is specific to the two deployed draft architectures (DFlash2 and DSPARK), which are unusual production drafts.
- **Only two targets, two benchmarks, one domain.** Both are coding agents on software-engineering tasks. The claim's scope is explicitly "long-horizon agents", but the evidence covers DeepSWE and NL2Repo-Bench only, on GLM-5.3 and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash.
- **Measurement depends on the deployment stack.** The whole precision story is tied to NVFP4/FP8/quantized-KV/sparse-attention serving on B200 GPUs with SGLang, and the DSPARK draft had to be ported from inference-only kernels to differentiable PyTorch to be trainable at all (matching reference kernels to within 1e-7 per expert and 3–5% per stage output). Transfer to a different serving stack is untested.
- **Earlier briefings understated the evidence; the "30 steps" figure holds.** The archived 5 November briefing recorded that no table-level effect sizes had been verified from the full text. The inspected text does report them (the three DeepSWE / NL2Repo-Bench gains above), and the abstract's claim that draft acceptance "can change the output distribution of the model after 30 steps" matches the measured finding that the long-horizon excess "sets in after about 30 turns". The number to cite is 30; the measured phenomenon is turn-based, and the paper does not report a step-count threshold at which the output distribution changes.

## Competing approaches

The paper's related work separates four lines. *Speculative decoding and draft training*: speculative decoding itself (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023), Medusa (Cai et al., 2024), EAGLE and EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2024, 2025) drafting from the target's hidden features, DFlash (Chen et al., 2026b) drafting a block in parallel with a block-diffusion model, DistillSpec (Zhou et al., 2024) distilling the target into the draft, and DSpark (Cheng et al., 2026). In all of these the draft is trained to *agree* with the target and the only metric is decoding speed; ASR trains the draft toward a different goal — to disagree with the target at chosen positions.

*Relaxing exactness*: Judge Decoding (Bachmann et al., 2025) accepts draft tokens a learned judge considers high quality even when the target would reject them, trading fidelity to the target for speed. ASR moves in the opposite direction: the verification rule and its exactness guarantee stay intact.

*Numerical nondeterminism in LLM inference*: Yuan et al. (2025) show reasoning models vary by several accuracy points across system configurations under bfloat16 with greedy decoding; He and Lab (2025) trace much run-to-run variation to non-batch-invariant kernels; quantized KV caches (Liu et al., 2024) and sparse attention (Liu et al., 2025) add approximation errors on top. These studies treat precision as a property of the whole system and try to remove the variation; ASR instead measures how precision varies *within* a verification block and exploits that variation.

*Error accumulation and critical tokens in long-horizon agents*: SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2024) as the canonical long-horizon agent benchmark; Sinha et al. (2026) on self-conditioning (models more likely to error once their context contains their own earlier mistakes, which larger models alone do not remove); Lin et al. (2025) on identifying and replacing critical tokens with token-level contrastive estimation; Wang et al. (2025) on ~20% high-entropy chain-of-thought tokens acting as forks and restricting RL updates to them; Li et al. (2026) (AgentBaton) on critical reasoning in long-horizon tasks needing stronger models. The authors' differentiation: Lin et al. and Wang et al. change the *target* model, whereas ASR applies DPO only to the draft, in reverse.

Within this knowledge base the comparison is at the level of **where capability is optimised**: model inference here, external harness in [[projects/envopt]], information environment in [[projects/roboarchitect]]. These are analyst-level **compares_with** links, not shared implementations, and ASR is not an instance of [[techniques/multi-agent-orchestration]]. See [[evaluations/briefing-synthesis-2026-11-03-to-09]] for the broader cross-briefing synthesis.

## Implications

The following is analysis by the KB maintainers, not a claim of the authors.

The most transferable idea is that **acceptance is not only a throughput knob**. Standard speculative-decoding work optimises accepted tokens per step; ASR shows that the *position* within a block at which a token is committed is itself a controllable degree of freedom over numerical precision. For anyone running a self-hosted speculative stack, the immediately actionable consequence is measurement, not training: instrument log-probability drift by verification slot on your own stack and see whether the ~4%-per-slot slope and the slot-0-is-as-precise-as-decoding property hold for your kernels, quantisation and attention implementation. If they do, the control channel exists in your deployment even if you never train an adversarial draft.

Second, the deployment arithmetic is attractive: the intervention trains a small fraction of the *draft* (87% of a 2.46B draft for GLM-5.3; 3.2% of a 14.2B draft for DeepSeek) and 0.28%/0.06% of the deployed system, leaves the target weights and the verification rule untouched, and costs at most ~2% of accepted draft tokens per block under the paper's approximation. For a team that cannot touch the target model — because it is served, licensed or frozen — this is a rare intervention on a layer the target does not own. The corollary is that it is also a technique that only applies where you own the draft.

Third, the **reverse-direction training is the surprising and reusable part**. Training a draft to prefer the *worse* option works only because rejection sampling is exact: the draft cannot make the target produce more desirable reasoning, only change *where* the target's own choice is committed. This is a general pattern worth naming — in any pipeline where a cheap component can influence an expensive one only through a rejection/resampling channel, the cheap component's *disagreement* can be a control signal without being a correction. It also explains the paper's cleanest control: drafts trained in the preferred direction score *below* the unbiased draft.

Fourth, the paper separates three things that are usually conflated — whether a draft can be trained to prefer something (yes, 0.635 and 0.85 held-out), whether it can prefer something *semantically correct* (no, at chance) — and uses that separation to argue a draft need not be smarter, only differently-phrased. For engineer-facing tooling this is a useful design discipline: define the signal you want the cheap component to carry narrowly enough that it is learnable.

Fifth, the practical instrumentation advice for anyone running agents on hosted APIs: you cannot apply this, but you can apply the *diagnostic*. Log draft acceptance, restart boundaries and downstream task errors across long trajectories; if long-horizon failures correlate with where in a verification block a decision token was committed, that is the mechanism ASR claims, and it is invisible to ordinary accuracy metrics.

## Reproducible experiment

No artifact release exists for the trained drafts or the fork-pair datasets, so any check means reimplementation from the method section. A feasible independent test of the core claim:

1. **Slot-wise precision measurement (no training needed).** On your own serving stack, hold the prefix fixed in memory, repeat only the forward decode pass R = 4 times on the unchanged KV cache, and compare log-probabilities at each verification slot, following the paper's method exactly. The reference is: slot 0 as noisy as single-token decoding (0.473 vs 0.478 nats on GLM-5.3), a slope of about +0.0195 nats per slot, and the last slot 34% noisier, with the >2-nat fraction rising from 3.2% to 6.2%. A flat profile would falsify the premise that slot position determines encoding precision on your stack; a steeper profile would mean the opportunity is larger there.
2. **Matched-budget adversarial vs forward vs unrelated drafts.** On a self-hosted speculative stack with a draft/target pair, train three drafts on the same fork pairs: (A) adversarial, (B) forward (chosen and rejected sides swapped), (C) a draft trained on an unrelated preference with a comparable rejection rate. Hold target weights, prompt set, hardware, numerical precision, sampling parameters and the task-scoring harness fixed, and run each configuration at least five times independently. The paper's prediction is a specific *ordering*, not just a gain: A > unbiased > B (GLM-5.3: 67.6% > 60.6% > 55.7%), with C indistinguishable from unbiased (pooled 67.5% vs 67.8%). Reproducing only the "A beats unbiased" half of that ordering would leave the mechanism claim open; reproducing the whole ordering is what distinguishes the ASR explanation from "any perturbation of the draft helps".
3. **Does placement matter beyond rejection rate?** Measure the rejection-position distribution, accepted draft-token runs, verification-window boundaries and finite-precision divergence, and check whether gains correlate with *where* rejections land rather than with the aggregate acceptance rate. The paper's efficiency figures (GLM-5.3 slot moves of +0.1 to −0.4 points, DeepSeek slot moves of −0.4 to −1.2 points, expected accepted tokens per block 2.28 → 2.28 and 2.46 → 2.5) give the reference for how much acceptance may drop without a throughput penalty — and they double as the acceptance-rate matching you need for condition (C).
4. **Precision-error sensitivity at deployment settings.** Repeat the measurement at several precision configurations (bf16 vs FP8 KV cache vs quantized weights) to check the paper's claim that the effect is a property of the *serving stack* rather than of the model, and quantify the nucleus-membership sensitivity at your actual temperature and top-p (the paper's numbers for top-p 0.95–1.0 at temperature 1: 12% of cases for a 1–2 nat shift, 47% for 2–3 nats, over 84% beyond 3 nats).

Substituting available models for the paper's hosted 2026 backbones is unavoidable; the substitutions, precision settings, hardware and draft architecture should be reported alongside the results.
