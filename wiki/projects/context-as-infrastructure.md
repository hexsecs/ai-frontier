# Context as Infrastructure

## What it is

"Context as Infrastructure" (Yu et al., arXiv:2610.41172, submitted 30 October 2026) proposes **knowledge compilation**: an LLM-driven ingestion pipeline that pre-processes a corpus once, upstream of any query, into typed artifacts — navigation trees whose leaves are atomic claims with verbatim evidence, entity–relation graphs, timelines, mind maps, ontology labels, synthesized wiki articles, durable memory — which an agentic search harness then consumes instead of raw text. The authors call harness plus compiler "an agent-native database: a store that hands the agent compiled, governed context instead of a ranked list of passages."

The paper is a vendor paper and should be read as one: 17 of its 18 authors are at InfiniFlow Inc. of Shanghai (the remaining author is at East China Normal University), and InfiniFlow is the company behind RAGFlow, the open-source system the paper describes. What makes the page worth having is that the paper is unusually deflationary about its own proposal. Its core thesis is that "retrieval is a harness problem before it is an index problem," and it states the point flatly in the abstract: "Compilation is not a better retriever" — it is "a supply layer." The authors' own framing baseline is that the accuracy problem is already largely solved without any compilation at all:

> "a harness whose only retrieval tools are BM25 and grep, with MiniMax M3 as the answering model, answers 85% of BrowseComp-Plus and reaches 91% on the multi-hop FRAMES benchmark … without any compiled index and without any vector index."

The problem compilation is meant to solve is therefore not accuracy but process cost: an undisciplined agentic loop "rewrites the same keywords, rereads the same document, piles dozens of marginal passages into the context, and finally bets on the model's patience for an answer." The measurements that follow largely sustain that deflation. No compiled structure the authors tested improved question-answering accuracy detectably. The one intervention that measured a real effect — claim–evidence substitution at query time — is an economic intervention on token consumption, not a retrieval improvement, and it comes with a latency price the paper also reports. The paper's quantitative claims are scoped by its authors to English text corpora, BM25/grep harnesses, and question answering; everything said about ontologies, graphs, wikis, timelines, mind maps, and memory is placed on a separate deployment ledger that, as detailed below, contains no measured effects at all.

## Mechanism

Formally (§3.1), knowledge compilation is a query-independent transformation κ: D → A = {a₁,…,aₘ} from a document set to typed artifacts, where each artifact is "a typed, versioned function of source documents that preserves a lineage link to the exact spans from which it was derived."

**Compilation (offline, once per document version).** The ingestion pipeline has six replaceable stages. *Parse* converts PDF, Office, HTML, Markdown, tables, and logs into a canonical representation preserving headings, lists, tables, and page anchors. *Chunk* splits at natural boundaries — and deliberately uses the same chunks the uncompiled baseline harness sees, so "compilation is not confounded with chunking." *Enrich* runs a model over each chunk in document context to emit artifacts: atomic claims with evidence, summary labels, entities and relations, events and dates, ontology labels, or a synthesized article; the extractor in every experiment is the small model glm-4-flash ("we did not need a frontier model"). *Validate* checks evidence spans against the source, entity types against the ontology, dates against the document, and units against the template schema — "Invalid units are dropped, not repaired by the model." *Assemble* clusters claims into a carrier tree, resolves entities into a graph, orders events into a timeline, and links articles into a wiki. *Register* stores each artifact with its source version, offsets, extractor version, validation status, and access-control labels — the step that makes "fallback, citation, audit, and incremental update possible." Seven templates (Tree, Graph, Ontology, Wiki, Timeline, Mind-map, Memory) share the parser, validator, and registry, so a deployment can switch templates "without re-ingesting." A channel-separation rule keeps raw and derived material apart: "Derived artifacts are read-only, typed, and never written back into the source," which the authors say "prevents the most common failure of LLM-maintained corpora — a generated summary becoming the 'source' for the next generation."

**The measured artifact: claim–evidence pairs.** The only structure carried through controlled measurement is the claim–evidence pair, a unit u = ⟨c, e, s, τ⟩ consisting of a normalized claim c, a verbatim evidence span e, the source chunk and character offsets s, and validity metadata τ such as document time. Construction is extract–validate–assemble: a small model proposes atomic, decontextualized claims per chunk with the supporting sentence or clause copied verbatim; an exact-presence span check then discards any claim whose span cannot be located — "the model may propose, but the source disposes" — and surviving claims are embedded and clustered hierarchically into a tree whose internal nodes are navigational labels, not summaries, and whose leaves are the claim–evidence pairs. The design motto is "lossy in prose and lossless in provenance": the claim text is a normalized rewrite, but the evidence is copied exactly and carries a pointer back to the source.

The emphasis matters, because the paper is explicit that the tree is not where the measured effect lives: "The tree is a carrier and an organizing device, not the retrieval mechanism." The mechanism is what happens at serving time.

**Serving (online, per query).** The paper defines three consumption modes. In **E1, evidence-first retrieval**, the harness searches an index of the evidence units directly — BM25 over claim text, anchored on chunks that lexical search already ranks — and receives evidence units as candidates. The alternative, descending from a document into its tree, lost: "the document was usually found correctly, the tree did not disambiguate within it, and the extra hop consumed a turn. Hierarchy is carrier, not retriever." In **E2, evidence substitution** — the default serving mode and the one behind the headline economics — the harness searches raw text exactly as the uncompiled baseline does, but when it localizes a chunk, a projector replaces the chunk with that chunk's claim–evidence pairs before content enters the context window, and a gate decides whether to answer from the evidence or open the raw chunk. In **E3, decision-gated reading**, a lightweight decision model (a Qwen decision-model preview service in the reported configuration) inspects the localized chunk and the question and decides whether a deep read is warranted; it controls reading depth rather than candidate order, and "without evidence, it saves about 30% of tokens."

**The harness itself** runs five sequential stations: a capability check (structural tools are not even exposed if the corpus carries no compiled artifacts); an evidence prefetch (one corpus-wide flat evidence recall before the model decides how to search); an evidence gate (sufficiency judged "by count, source diversity, and coverage of the question" — if sufficient, one model call answers and no raw text is read; if not, "the whole block is discarded rather than stuffed into the prompt"); a tool loop of parallel retrieval actions under a step limit and wall-clock budget; and a deterministic close that composes an answer from evidence in hand on abnormal termination. Six invariants govern the loop, among them "evidence before thought," citations assigned at delivery rather than model-generated, and a guaranteed close. A zero-hit discipline handles empty results: "A zero-hit result is a property of the tool, not a fact about the corpus," so the harness explains the zero and retries a fallback ladder — relax the filter, fall back from compiled to raw lexical search, widen the time window — before the model may conclude that something is absent. The retrieval stack itself is BM25 plus grep over chunks, with a lexical claim-text leg; there is no dense or vector leg anywhere in the system.

## Evidence

All results are author-reported — the paper's own phrasing is "in our runs" — and are single-run descriptive aggregates: no confidence intervals, no repeated seeds (§8.8). Token and accuracy figures for the main comparison (Table 4) are measured on N = 200 FRAMES questions; the structural audit (Table 3) covers the full FRAMES benchmark with each variant run once; the latency comparison rests on 7 paired questions. FRAMES is a multi-hop question-answering benchmark over Wikipedia with entity-linked evidence; BrowseComp-Plus is used only as a harness scale check. The authors warn that the two benchmarks' figures "are not comparable."

**The framing baseline.** The uncompiled BM25/grep harness with MiniMax M3 scores 91.0% accuracy on FRAMES at an average of 324,479 tokens per query and 46.7 s latency, and 85.0% on BrowseComp-Plus at roughly 3.0×10⁶ tokens per question. As the authors put it: "The harness-only configuration reaches 85% on BrowseComp-Plus with BM25, grep, and MiniMax M3, at roughly three million tokens per question, and 91% on FRAMES without any compiled index." External work they cite points the same way: tuned BM25 with a capable agent reaches 83.1% on BrowseComp-Plus (Hsu et al. 2026).

**The negative headline: structure does not buy accuracy.** Auditing structural variants against the uncompiled harness on FRAMES, the paper concludes: "On FRAMES, no compiled variant produced an accuracy improvement large enough to be distinguished from run-to-run variation in a single run." A RAPTOR-style summary tree (claims removed) was neutral — the "agent used summaries to orient, then grepped anyway." An LLM wiki was neutral, with "lossy provenance and exact wording; expensive to rebuild." An entity–relation graph was neutral, because FRAMES supplies entity-linked evidence and few of its questions require a join. A PageIndex-style outline was neutral at small scale and **negative at FRAMES scale**: "Extracted hierarchy incomplete or misaligned; a wrong branch cost more than open search; confident routing concentrated queries on few documents." The authors' scale argument is analytic rather than measured: an outline 95% correct per routing decision with branching factor eight yields only "about 75% end-to-end routing success at 10⁵ documents." The weakest option measured anywhere in the paper is the one common in long-context RAG — letting the harness read a navigation tree's table of contents and letting the LLM choose which documents to read — described as "very low accuracy at very high token cost," with no figures reported.

**The one measured win: substitution economics.** Conditional on the gate's verdict, evidence substitution changes total query tokens by Δ_hit = −0.91 when the substituted evidence suffices and Δ_miss = +0.019 when it does not — in the abstract's words, a tree "whose leaves are claim–evidence pairs … cuts total query tokens by 91% when the evidence suffices and adds 1.9% when it does not, for a 38% average reduction." The average on FRAMES is −38% tokens at no reliable accuracy change. The asymmetry is the result: because a miss costs almost nothing, the break-even sufficiency rate — the fraction of queries for which evidence must suffice for substitution to pay — is p_h* = 0.019/(0.91 + 0.019) ≈ 2.0%. The authors call this "the central economic fact of the mechanism." The sufficiency rate implied by the −38% average is p̂_h ≈ 43% (back-solved from the endpoints, not counted directly). The paper's sensitivity analysis shows the break-even is governed by the miss penalty: ±1.5 pp of uncertainty in the +1.9% miss figure moves p_h* between roughly 0.4% and 3.6%, while the same uncertainty in the hit saving moves it only between 2.0% and 2.1% — so "the quantity a team should measure carefully is the frequency of failed projections."

**The decision gate, and substitution's latency price.** Adding the decision gate to evidence gives an arm mean of −52% tokens (155,305 tokens per query on average) and cuts end-to-end latency from 140 s to 30 s, again with no reliable accuracy change; the gate without evidence saves about 30% of tokens "by declining deep reads that did not change the answer." The authors note the gate's marginal contribution over substitution alone is "modest because evidence is already compact." But substitution itself is not latency-free. In a paired ablation on the 7 questions both arms answered, "the claim page roughly halved query tokens while end-to-end latency roughly tripled, and the mechanism is turns, not prompt size": smaller pages are more often incomplete, so the agent takes more turns. "Evidence substitution therefore buys tokens with turns: the same property that makes a page small makes it incomplete." The 140 s → 30 s gate figure and this tripling are not in tension in the paper's account — the gate's job is precisely to suppress the extra reads that drive the turn count — but the two numbers come from different arms and different question sets, and the paper presents them in different sections.

Two accounting caveats belong with the evidence. First, p_h is an *acceptance* rate — the gate answering from evidence — not a measured sufficiency rate; wrongly accepted evidence (a false-accept rate φ) would silently tax accuracy. The paper specifies an estimator and an audit of 100–400 labeled accepted queries, and then states plainly that the audit was not run: "Until that audit is run, Eq. (11) remains uncalibrated, and we report no value of φ." Second, the serving ablations behind the retained configuration (topN = 20 anchored chunks, k = 100; anchor depth 10 → 20 helped, 30 did not; fixed page caps hurt recall; query–claim reordering did not help) are reported as directions only, by design.

## Limitations

**Compilation build cost is never measured.** The paper's economics are an amortization argument: compilation costs c query-equivalents (C = c·T₀), and the payback horizon is N* = c/(0.929·p_h − 0.019), so at the implied p_h ≈ 0.43 a corpus "whose compilation costs ten query-equivalents pays back after about 26 queries." But c enters the model only as a free parameter; the value 10 is an illustrative assumption, and no RAGFlow ingestion cost — tokens, dollars, or wall time — for compiling FRAMES or any other corpus is reported anywhere in the paper. The amortization conclusion therefore cannot be checked against the authors' own deployment: a reader cannot learn from this paper what compilation actually costs on the system the paper is about. A finite-horizon correction (a pilot with Q = 1,000 queries at c = 10 needs p_h > 3.12%, not 2%) sharpens the formula without supplying the missing measurement.

**The deployment ledger is empty.** Every artifact family other than claim–evidence pairs — ontology, graph, wiki, timeline, mind map, memory — is reported on a pre-registered deployment ledger (Table 2) of metric designs rather than results. The paper states the outcome without hedging:

> "No row of this ledger has yet met its pre-registered target in an instrumented deployment, so every row is reported as *no measured effect* and none is promoted to a general claim."

So the ontology's canonical-class mapping rate, the timeline's ordering accuracy and stale-version errors, and the auditability target (delivered claims with resolvable span and current source version must be 100%) are specified but unmeasured. The compiled structures this paper is titled after are, on its own accounting, carrier and organizational devices with a token-economics result for one of them — not demonstrated retrieval or deployment improvements.

**The no-dense-retrieval stance is asserted, never quantified.** The all-lexical design rests on the claim that "adding a dense leg lowered accuracy in our runs," which appears three times (§1, §8.1, §9) with no magnitude, table, or configuration. It justifies the absence of any vector-depth ablation — "There is no vector-depth ablation to report, because the harness has no dense leg to widen" — so the most consequential architectural commitment in the system is the one with no numbers behind it in the paper.

**Staleness is handled architecturally and measured never.** The design story is coherent: artifacts are registered with source versions; the store is "versioned and content-addressed, so a claim resolves to the exact document version that produced it"; delivery checks that claims resolve to "a live source version"; when a document changes, "only its chunks are re-extracted, its claim set replaced, and affected clusters updated"; and the authors acknowledge that an LLM wiki "goes stale unless recompiled." What is missing is any measurement: no re-compilation cost under churn, no accuracy or token figures on a changing corpus, no refresh policy (triggering or scheduling), and both benchmarks used are static snapshots. The expense and benefit of keeping compiled artifacts fresh — the central operational question for "context as infrastructure" — are unmeasured.

**The authors' own threats to validity (§8.8)** cover much of the rest. "Our measurements are descriptive aggregates from one benchmark and one compiler model. We report no confidence intervals and no repeated seeds, so the 91%/1.9% endpoints are measured operating points, not population estimates." "FRAMES is English and Wikipedia-based; enterprise corpora with tables, scans, and jargon may have lower extraction quality, raising the miss rate and reducing savings." The PageIndex degradation "is an observation at FRAMES scale, not a general law," and the 75% routing figure is "an analytic model rather than a measured scale sweep." On generalization: "We report one compiler and one answering model, so the qualitative conclusion — evidence helps cost, structure does not help recall — is what we expect to generalize, not the exact percentages." Two further inspection-level caveats: the token savings are measured against an already-strong harness baseline, so savings relative to the conventional dense-RAG stack most deployments actually run are unknown; and the implied sufficiency rate p̂_h ≈ 43% is derived from the −38% average and the two endpoints, so the comfortable margin over the 2% break-even inherits the endpoints' combined error rather than being an independent count.

## Competing approaches

**Classical dense retrieval / standard RAG.** The paper positions the harness itself against passage-ranking RAG: once a harness writes queries, reads, reformulates, and decides when to stop, a ranked list of passages is the wrong interface. Against dense legs specifically, it offers only the unquantified assertion discussed above, plus cited related work on lexical sufficiency — direct corpus interaction outperforming dense, sparse, and reranking baselines on BrowseComp-Plus and multi-hop QA (Li et al. 2026), the "Is Grep All You Need?" line of work, and the tuned-BM25 result of 83.1% (Hsu et al. 2026). No dense configuration is run inside the authors' own harness.

**Long-context and table-of-contents selection.** The weakest measured option in the paper is letting the model read a navigation tree's ToC and choose documents itself — "very low accuracy at very high token cost," unquantified. The harness's flat prefetch and evidence gate are offered as the disciplined alternative.

**RAPTOR-style summary trees** tie the baseline on accuracy; the agent used the summaries to orient and then grepped anyway. **PageIndex-style outlines** tie at small scale and lose at FRAMES scale, with the compounding-routing-error model as the explanation. **LLM wikis** tie on accuracy with worse provenance and expensive rebuilds. **Entity–relation graphs and GraphRAG-style indexes** tie on FRAMES, where the benchmark hands the system entity-linked evidence and few questions need a join; the paper deliberately omits GraphRAG's community reports, arguing they "fragment knowledge and add a token-heavy layer of generated text," and at query time uses PageRank over chunks followed by claim–evidence fetch instead. **Query-time compression (LLMLingua-style)** is contrasted by construction rather than by experiment: compilation is "corpus-level and query-independent: the compression is paid once, reused, and keeps a pointer to the exact source span" — no head-to-head numbers are reported. **SIRA-style index enrichment** with LLM-generated evidence vocabulary is cited as prior art for collapsing iterative search into one lexical call, again without a head-to-head.

The competitive claim that survives the paper's own audit is narrow: structure-only alternatives tie on accuracy, and the only measured advantage anywhere in the comparison set is substitution's token saving, bought with latency.

## Implications

For builders of agentic retrieval, the paper's most transferable content is its baseline, not its proposal: a disciplined harness over BM25 and grep — with a sufficiency gate, a zero-hit fallback ladder, budgeted tool calls, and delivery-time citation assignment — reached 91% on FRAMES before any compilation was added. Teams evaluating compiled-context products, including RAGFlow itself, should read the −38% figure as a claim about one mechanism (substituting verbatim-anchored claim–evidence pairs for raw chunks at read time) under one gate, not as a property of knowledge compilation generally; the same paper reports no accuracy effect for the structures that give compilation its name. The economic formula is genuinely reusable, and its lesson is about what to instrument: the saving is robust to error in the hit rate and fragile to error in the miss penalty, so the frequency of failed projections — the gate accepting evidence that does not suffice — is the number to measure first, ideally with the false-accept audit the paper specifies but does not run.

For evaluators, the paper is a useful template and a useful warning at once. It pre-registers deployment metrics, reports an empty ledger as empty, separates acceptance from correctness, and states its reversal conditions (non-English or scan/table-heavy corpora, short query horizons against expensive compilation, hard latency budgets: "When latency is a hard constraint, those token savings may not be worth buying"). It also shows how a paper can do all that and still leave its central cost parameter unmeasured and its most load-bearing architectural assertion unquantified. Within this knowledge base, the span-validation discipline — claims stored only with a verbatim, locatable evidence span — is the concrete bridge to [[techniques/verification-and-evidence]], and the substitution gate's acceptance-versus-sufficiency gap is a clean instance of the evaluation hazard treated in [[techniques/evaluation-and-simulation]].

## Reproducible experiment

Three checks are extractable, at increasing cost.

**1. Arithmetic audit (no code).** The cost model can be verified from the reported endpoints alone: E[Δ] = 0.019 − 0.929·p_h; the break-even p_h* = 0.019/0.929 ≈ 2.0%; the implied sufficiency rate p̂_h = 0.399/0.929 ≈ 43% from the −38% average; and the payback N* ≈ 2.6c at p_h = 0.43. All reproduce from the text.

**2. Artifact-level check with RAGFlow.** RAGFlow is open source (see Evidence status). Ingest a small document set with the Tree template in the paper's Listing 1 configuration — glm-4-flash extraction, `require_verbatim_span: true`, span-match validation — and test the paper's central representation claim directly against the store: every stored claim's evidence span should match the source verbatim at the recorded chunk offsets, and claims with unlocatable spans should be absent (dropped at validation, not repaired). This checks the "lossless in provenance" property without needing the authors' harness.

**3. Mini token-economics run.** Take roughly 20 multi-hop questions over a small Wikipedia subset and run a BM25/grep harness with evidence substitution off versus on, recording per-query token deltas split by the gate's hit/miss verdict plus the acceptance rate. The paper predicts the *shape* — a large saving on hits, a ~2% penalty on misses, tokens down while rounds and latency rise — rather than any specific average at small N. Exact reproduction of Table 4 is not expected to be possible: it requires the authors' harness configuration and the Qwen decision-model preview service, and the paper reports single-run operating points, so a replication should compare signs and rough magnitudes, not the −38% point value. None of these checks was performed for this page; they are specified from the paper's own configuration and formulas.

## Evidence status

Everything quantitative on this page is **author-reported**: all experiments were run by the paper's authors, 17 of 18 of whom are employees of InfiniFlow, the vendor of the system evaluated. There is no independent replication, and the metrics have not been independently reverified. The paper's internal labels have been preserved rather than flattened: benchmark accuracies (harness baseline, substitution and gate arms) are single-run operating points from the authors' runs; the deployment-ledger claims for ontology, graph, wiki, timeline, mind-map, and memory artifacts are the authors' own "no measured effect"; the dense-retrieval claim is an unquantified assertion by the authors; and the PageIndex scale figure is the authors' analytic model, not a measurement.

On availability: no dataset release accompanies the paper, and no compiled FRAMES artifacts, harness configurations, or run logs are published; FRAMES and BrowseComp-Plus are third-party public benchmarks. The system itself is implemented in the open-source RAGFlow project (github.com/infiniflow/ragflow), which the paper links — but that repository was **not cloned, executed, or tested** for this page, and no claim here rests on inspecting it. The decision gate additionally depends on external hosted services (a Qwen decision-model preview and, in the authors' comparison, StartLux), which are cited as services, not artifacts.
