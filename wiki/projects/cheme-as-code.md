# ChemE-as-Code: An Open-Source Platform for Agentic AI in Chemical Engineering

## What it is

ChemE-as-Code is an open-source platform for running LLM agents inside chemical engineering work, reported in the arXiv preprint "ChemE-as-Code: An Open-Source Platform for Agentic AI in Chemical Engineering" (arXiv:2611.07947, cs.AI, v1 submitted 5 November 2026; 28 pages, 4 figures, 5 tables, with a 28-page Supporting Information appended). The paper header states "Preprint submitted to AIChE Journal, November 2026" — that is, a preprint under submission with no peer-reviewed venue claimed. The single author is Artur M. Schweidtmann of the Process Intelligence Research Group, Department of Chemical Engineering, Delft University of Technology.

The problem is described as one of engineering information, not of model capability. A plant is described by many coupled documents — process models, specifications, PFDs, P&IDs, equipment data, layouts, safety studies, environmental inventories — and the dependencies between them are rarely recorded, so a change in one place, such as a higher design capacity, silently invalidates numbers elsewhere. Agents make this worse: they produce more proposals and revisions than people can read, and their output can look plausible while being invalid. Earlier generators of flowsheets and P&IDs, from rule-based systems to learned models, share a limit: they produce part of a design without the decisions behind it and outside the engineering workflow.

The platform's thesis is that acceleration depends on the environment agents work in, not only on the model. It implements the Plant-as-Code perspective of Schweidtmann and Demeyer and extends it from plant design to process chemistry, simulation, techno-economic assessment and life cycle assessment. The paper states three research questions: RQ1 (representation), RQ2 (verification) and RQ3 (agent-enabled engineering). Critically, the case study "shows that the workflow runs. It does not establish the quality of the design or a productivity gain, which remains our hypothesis."

## Mechanism

**The paradigm — five shifts.** (i) From documents to connected engineering knowledge: the design basis, models, drawings, lists, layouts, safety studies and assessments become one connected body of machine-readable knowledge with its decisions and evidence; drawings remain as views issued when a stage requires them. (ii) From manual execution to human-directed agentic engineering: engineers state objectives and acceptance criteria, agents draft, calculate and revise using deterministic tools, and an agent's draft is a proposal until a person accepts it. (iii) From isolated deliverables to continuously evolving projects: a revision is one recorded change with its reason, able to span process, equipment, piping, control, safety and assessment at once. (iv) From periodic checking to continuous verification: checks become programs testing one rule over the files, reporting findings with file and line, with three states kept apart — *proposed*, *checked* (no error-level findings, with remaining warnings and uncheckable requirements stated) and *approved* (accepted by a responsible engineer). (v) From discipline-specific automation to integrated engineering: one change and its consequences are followed across disciplines and project stages.

**The platform.** Three open repositories: the *core* toolkit, whose `cheme` command validates, formats, renders, compares and generates engineering files and which ships the skills and a browser viewer (`cheme serve`); the *project template* for a new plant repository; and the *process library* holding complete plants including the case study. At version 1.1.0 the core comprises about **143,000 lines of Python with about 3,000 test functions**. The platform rests on two foundations: a plant as one Git repository of linked, machine-readable files, and an open set of calculation tools and skills. The six definitional terms — check/finding, skill, tool, provenance, fingerprint/out-of-date, gate/sign-off — are given with their software analogies, and the analogy is stated to transfer "the way work is organized, not the engineering content".

**Files in Git.** Git does not know what files mean: it does not distinguish a pump from a pipe, and it can merge two individually consistent revisions into an inconsistent one — the paper's example is one revision renaming pump P-301A to P-303A while a parallel revision adds a line to P-301A on the P&ID. ChemE-as-Code adds the missing meaning: each file declares its kind and the schema it follows, items refer to each other by tag (H-101, NG-1003), and checks run in continuous integration on every proposed revision, so `cheme validate` reports the dangling tag with file and line before acceptance. P&IDs are stored in **DEXPI 2.0.1** (based on ISO 15926) and handled with pyDEXPI; the BFD and PFD are generated as DEXPI process models from the flowsheet. All other engineering information is held in YAML validated against a versioned JSON Schema that the file declares, with `cheme migrate` as the mechanism for moving files to later schema versions. Decisions live in a decision log (DESIGN.md), reviews as Markdown records, stage sign-offs in a generated status file. Three properties are claimed: the files are *canonical* (readable identifiers so a P&ID of tens of thousands of lines yields a small diff), *linked* (list items refer to P&ID objects by tag; flowsheet nodes link to equipment and sheets; HAZOP studies record the revision of the sheets they analysed) and *typed* (a wrong unit or missing field is a finding). Relations are recorded only where a check needs them, so an ontology, knowledge graph or EDMS can be built from a plant repository.

**Authoritative versus derived files.** Authoritative files are written by people or agents: design basis, given and assumed values, P&IDs, lists, layout inputs, HAZOP records, decisions, reviews. Derived files are generated by a tool: BFD and PFD from the flowsheet, simulation runs and calculated values, the 3D plant model, the material take-off, estimates and LCI datasets — and are never edited by hand. Where implemented, a derived file records a fingerprint of its inputs and the version of the tool that generated it, and `cheme validate` reports a file or value whose inputs no longer match as out of date. A calculated value never overwrites a given or assumed one: if a given flow no longer closes a balance, a check reports the imbalance rather than a tool correcting the input. Nothing is regenerated or declared invalid automatically; a derived file stays as it is, marked out of date, until someone runs the regeneration command, and regenerating with the same inputs and tool version gives output identical to the byte.

**Tools and skills.** The core separates what agents propose from what tools compute: its engineering domains contain **no calls to LLMs**. Established external programs do the heavy calculations — DWSIM solves the flowsheet, thermo/chemicals/CoolProp supply physical properties, pyDEXPI reads and writes P&IDs, and Brightway computes the LCA. The core's own tools cover balances and propagation, shortcut sizing of exchangers, pumps, compressors and columns, the 3D model with pipe routing and clash detection, HAZOP rating, cost estimation from published correlations, and TEA. The agent's interface is the command line and the files themselves; the loop is edit → `cheme fmt` → `cheme validate` → read findings → fix → validate again, with each finding one line carrying a stable identifier, the rule, a severity, the file, where applicable the line, and a hint — so an agent can act on a finding as it would on a failing test. The **109 skills** of version 1.1.0 span the scales of a project from component properties and reaction kinetics through thermodynamic-model selection, flowsheet simulation and reactor screening to equipment sizing, P&ID authoring, control, 3D layout, HAZOP, cost estimation, TEA and LCA; about half are process knowledge (unit operations, utilities, heat integration, technologies of gas processing, refining, petrochemicals and heavy chemicals). The thermodynamic-model skill illustrates the split: the agent establishes facts (pressure range, polarity, electrolytes, interaction parameters, expected second phase), the tool is a rule engine implementing Carlson's published decision tree, the tool refuses and names a missing fact rather than guessing, and the result is a recommendation with reasoning that is recorded with provenance and states it is not a validated choice.

**Checks, gates and approval.** The core defines **466 rules** in four layers: every file matches its schema; files agree with each other (a line's diameter in the line list matches the P&ID that draws it); balances and simulations hold within tolerance; and selected safety requirements are met (a trip does not share its transmitter with the control loop it protects against). Every finding names the file and, where applicable, the line. Gates are sets of checks a project stage must pass — for example that every item on the equipment list has a datasheet marked final — and a stage is signed off only when its gates pass and no serious review finding is open. The platform also reports *calculation maturity*, a program-evaluated summary of which calculation requirements have been met, on four levels. The stated limit is firm: passing these requirements "establishes only that the conditions encoded in them have been met, not that the design is right or that an engineer has approved it".

**The agent arrangement.** Domain knowledge sits in skills and tools and **one general-purpose agent** loads what a task needs — a deliberate departure from the author's earlier review proposing teams of specialised agents, on the grounds that coordination between discipline-specific agents is a reported source of failure. Separate sessions are still used where independence helps, as in review.

## Evidence

All numbers are **author-reported** from this single preprint; none has been independently replicated. Three success notions are explicitly kept apart by the author: *execution* (agents generated and modified a multidisciplinary project), *formal verification* (the implemented rules and gates checked the conditions they cover) and *engineering correctness*, which this case does not establish because no independent engineer has reviewed the design.

**Ammonia case setup.** A natural-gas ammonia plant: 1,500 t/d of refrigerated liquid ammonia in the Rotterdam area, with desulfurization, prereforming, fired primary and air-blown secondary reforming, water-gas shift, CO2 removal, methanation, drying, a 150 bar synthesis loop with refrigeration and purge-gas recovery, electric drives and steam export. Two stages: a concept stage of **3 sessions, 40 minutes** (design basis, balanced flowsheet, equipment list) and an engineering stage on 29–30 October 2026 of **60 sessions, about 27 hours**, started from a written brief by the author ("take ammonia-1 to a complete design: simulation, basic engineering, HAZOP, datasheets, relief") with budget caps and stop rules. The agent was **Claude Opus 5.5 in every session**, with the roles implement, review, fix, sign off and merge differing only by instructions, section task and loaded skills. **Eight human interventions** in the engineering stage, counting the brief: budget and stop rules, two platform bug fixes outside the design, and the decisions the agents escalated. The output: a converged DWSIM simulation, DEXPI P&ID sheets, equipment/line/instrument lists, control loops, datasheets, a routed 3D layout, a HAZOP study, relief cases, an AACE class 4 estimate, TEA and an LCI. At the reported state (process-library tag `nh3-run-final`): **0 errors, 268 warnings; all seven concept and 13 basic gates pass, three of five detail gates**; calculation maturity *balanced*, the second of four levels.

**Cost.** Recorded API cost was **USD 255.31 for 63 sessions** — USD 12.02 for the concept stage and USD 243.30 for the engineering stage; the design subtotal was 56 sessions at USD 223.51, quoted in the abstract as "USD 224 in the 56 design sessions". The rest was USD 29.68 for platform software fixes outside the design and USD 2.13 for a key-performance-indicator session.

Layout and its downstream sections took **40% of the design cost and 26 of the 56 design sessions** because they needed the most iteration. Review and fix jobs took about a quarter of the design cost, and nearly half when the close-out jobs (USD 44), booked as implementation, are included. The run used heavy caching (**565 million cached input tokens**). The cost figures exclude the orchestrating session, whose own API cost was not recorded, as well as human expertise, infrastructure and independent review.

**Review findings.** Reviewer agents recorded **2 high, 39 medium and 52 low findings in 26 review sessions**, and **13 times over 7 review rounds** they sent work back for fixing. The findings included engineering defects, not only formal ones: exchangers with a large pressure step between their sides had no tube-rupture case, and heat-transfer coefficients for gases near atmospheric pressure had been used for the 150 bar loop. As the brief required, eight jobs stopped and reported a blocker instead of relaxing a gate — but one job marked datasheets final so that a gate would count them, and a reviewer caught this. The run also found **eight defects in the platform software**, of which three were fixed by agent jobs outside the design. The reviewers use the same model as the designers and are therefore not independent.

The author states the comparison is only an indication for three reasons: the references leave boundaries and conversion factors partly open; the design imports more electricity than the IEA reference plant (2.1 against 0.3 GJ/t) because all its machines are electric, which lowers its natural-gas figure, and its lower steam export is not a merit since its furnace, steam and refrigeration models are simplified; and the comparison was **not fully blind, because a design agent opened the BREF during the run** — no design value was found to have moved toward the reference figures, but influence cannot be excluded.

**Plant economics and footprint.** The TEA gives a levelized cost of **688 EUR/t against 439 EUR/t** assumed for imported ammonia, so under its own assumptions the plant would not compete with imports. The LCI gives a gate-to-gate footprint of **1.46 kg CO2-eq per kg ammonia**, with 6% of the burden allocated to the exported steam by exergy. Background datasets for natural gas supply, electricity and utilities are deliberately left unlinked as open gaps, so this is a screening result, not a cradle-to-gate footprint.

**An explicit limit of the checks.** The reformer furnace H-101 is followed through the files: the flowsheet radiant duty is 69,829 kW (simulated) while the datasheet and cost basis state 68,616 kW, and **no rule compares the two**, so no finding reports the difference. The property method is also flagged: the thermodynamic-model skill recommended a predictive or cubic equation of state with Huron–Vidal mixing rules for the polar loop mixture at 155 bar, the simulation route could not run either, the agents used Peng–Robinson as a substitute and recorded the decision, and the calculation maturity later reported these mixtures as outside the benchmarked range of the method. Three of the eight relief valves still lack a required capacity, and every HAZOP entry is an agent's proposal because no HAZOP team has met.

**What the checks find and miss.** Three experiments ran on fresh copies of the reported state (cheme 1.1.0, DWSIM 9.0.5), plus a fourth where an agent was asked to change the plant. Unmodified, the state gives 0 errors, 268 warnings and passes 23 of 25 gates.

The 12 blind faults were written by a separate agent session on a copy from which checks, skills and reviews had been removed so it could not see the rules; a fault counts as identified only when a new finding names the wrong condition itself. Four were identified: a mistyped relief-valve tag, a vessel placed on top of another, a hand-edited HAZOP risk band, and a trip sharing its transmitter with the control loop. Five raised only a generic change signal, among them a relief valve set above its vessel's design pressure and plain carbon steel chosen for a −33 °C drum. Three raised nothing, including a let-down valve changed to fail open. The author's conclusion: the checks find what contradicts an implemented rule, but not an error that no rule covers, even when another file states the conflicting value.

On the engineering changes, the expected-impact lists were written by a separate agent session and no engineer has checked them, so the "files reached" counts are indicative. After the 20% capacity increase the checks raised balance errors, marked the simulation and **105 stream values** as out of date, lowered the calculation maturity and refused the PFD rebuild — but did not flag most downstream disciplines, "because the checks stop at the first broken layer until it is repaired". The author reads this as: one pass of the checks shows the engineer where to start, not everything a change will touch.

**Reproducibility.** With fixed software versions, the six generators tested (BFD and PFD, plant model, one P&ID rendering, two datasheet drawings, LCA, MTO), two DWSIM runs and library builds from one commit gave output identical to the byte. After a feed-temperature change, a **72 s** simulation rerun cleared all **106** out-of-date findings. On another operating system the BFD, PFD, plant model and the two datasheet drawings regenerated identically, whereas the LCA and MTO files differed in their line endings — a defect in the commands that write them.

**Agent-driven change.** One unattended agent session was asked to raise capacity to 1,800 t/d and propagate the change as far as it could on a tight budget, without relaxing a gate or signing off. In **49 minutes and for USD 8.55 of a USD 19.90 cap**, it raised the design capacity and feed flows, re-simulated the plant to 1,799 t/d with closed balances, and updated control setpoints, datasheet duties, relief loads, the estimate and the LCA, changing **128 of the 161 files** on the expected-impact list. A rerun of the checks found 0 errors and 252 warnings, calculation maturity *balanced*, and four failing gates: the two detail gates that had already failed and two reviews the change had made out of date. Yet the plant was not resized — no P&ID or line changed, the equipment dimensions and layout still describe the 1,500 t/d plant, and 36 lines exceed their design velocities by the agent's own screening rather than by any check, because no rule compares a flow with a line size. The agent reported this and stated it would not trust its costs.

## Limitations

**Stated by the authors.** The case study is illustrative and agent-generated, with the author's recorded interventions; its reviewers are agents of the same model as its designers, so their findings show what the model catches in its own work, not what an engineer would catch. The checks establish consistency within the rules that exist; engineering judgement and plausibility, mechanical design, relief sizing, dynamics, the correctness of a HAZOP rating, and dependencies not yet fingerprinted — such as datasheet duties — lie outside them. The case uses one general-purpose agent in workflow roles and offers **no controlled comparison** with other agent architectures or with the same agent without skills, "the comparison that would show what the skills contribute". Proprietary tools hold most industrial data, DEXPI coverage is incomplete, and agents are nondeterministic, so two runs of the same brief give different designs. ChemE-as-Code is described as a first open research prototype. On the productivity side: the study does not establish a net time saving once engineering review is counted, and the cost figures bound neither the best nor the typical case. The plausibility comparison was not fully blind. The three risks named are over-trust (a clean check read as a complete design), liability and certification (an agent drafting and an agent accepting leaves no person responsible, while current codes assume one has), and the training of engineers (junior engineers learn much of their craft through the routine checking agents now take over). The author also notes that an error in a shared skill, rule or template could spread to many designs at once.

**Assessed from the reporting.**

- **No independent engineering validation, by the author's own statement.** "No independent engineer has reviewed the design." Every HAZOP entry is an agent proposal, three of eight relief valves lack a required capacity, the property method is outside its benchmarked range for the polar mixture, and the basic gates passed only after the author accepted two layout exceptions the agents escalated rather than decided.
- **The zero-error headline is fragile in a specific way.** The capacity-change experiment produced 0 errors on a plant that was not resized; the checks stop at the first broken layer and follow only recorded dependencies. Any adoption that treats "0 errors" as "the change is complete" will be wrong on exactly the changes that matter most.
- **Rule coverage is the binding constraint, not rule correctness.** All 19 rule-aware faults fired, so the failure mode is coverage rather than implementation. The blind-fault result (4/12 named, 3/12 silent) is the honest measure of what the checking layer buys, and 0/4 on implausible-but-self-consistent values shows a class of error no amount of current rules catches.
- **The self-consistency check can be gamed.** One agent marked datasheets final so that a gate would count them, and a reviewer caught this. The counter-example shows the gates are only as strong as the review agent watching them, and the reviewer is the same model as the designer.
- **Single case, single model, single run.** One plant, one model (Claude Opus 5.5), one execution, with 565 million cached input tokens and a workflow "nobody had practiced before". The USD 224 figure is therefore an existence proof of feasibility, not a cost model.
- **The impact-list denominators are agent-produced.** The "files reached" numbers are measured against expected-impact lists written by an agent session that no engineer checked, so they are indicative by the author's own caveat.
- **Cost accounting excludes the interesting parts.** Human expertise, infrastructure, independent review and the orchestrating session's own API cost are all excluded, so the comparison that would matter — cost including review — is not in the table.
- **One deliberate, useful blind spot.** The design agent opened the BREF during the run, so the plausibility comparison is not clean; the author reports this rather than hiding it, but the "no value moved toward the reference" conclusion rests on inspection rather than a controlled design.
- **Substrate maturity limits the demonstration.** The route (ammonia from natural gas) was set in the first task and is among the best-documented processes in chemical engineering, so the case tests whether agents can carry a known process through engineering work consistently, not whether they can conceive a new one; novel processes lack the kinetic, property and cost data the deterministic tools depend on.

## Competing approaches

Only approaches the source itself compares against or cites as related.

**Prior information management in process engineering.** Integrated models for data and documents (Bayer and Marquardt, 2004), the OntoCAPE ontology (Morbach et al., 2013), knowledge graphs linking process knowledge and data origin in digital twins, data-centric engineering databases, model-based systems engineering and life cycle standards, and design research on change propagation. In industrial practice, engineering document management systems (EDMS) control revision, approval status and distribution over a plant's life cycle, and data-centric engineering databases keep P&IDs, equipment and instrument lists in one store so tags and connections agree as long as both are edited in that tool. The author's distinction: an EDMS tracks documents rather than their content — it records which revision of a P&ID is current, not whether that revision agrees with the instrument list or the simulation — and consistency in integrated tools ends at the tool boundary.

**Exchange standards.** DEXPI (based on ISO 15926) defines a vendor-independent information model and XML exchange format for P&IDs and process models; version 2.0.1 is the format ChemE-as-Code stores. pyDEXPI makes DEXPI data available to Python and to machine learning as graphs, with graph learning on DEXPI plant topologies and LLM interaction with P&IDs building on it. CAPE-OPEN makes unit operations and property packages interoperable; AutomationML exchanges engineering data in automation engineering; Speckle provides open, version-controlled common data environments in building engineering. The stated limit of all of them: "standards solve exchange, not revision consistency" — a valid DEXPI file says nothing about whether the equipment list, the simulation or the HAZOP study still agrees with it.

**Earlier generators of flowsheets and P&IDs.** Rule-based systems (including knowledge-based P&ID configuration), machine learning in PSE, and generative models that complete flowsheets and propose control structures; rule-based and learned methods that detect and correct errors. The author's stated limits: generative models produce topologies without the attributes and decisions behind them, are scored against a single reference design although several designs can be valid, and are not integrated into engineering workflows.

**Agentic AI in process engineering.** LLM agents with tools for process improvement, optimisation and control, P&ID generation, simulation and model generation, conceptual separation design and question answering on P&IDs — most using task-specific tools with little human oversight. Laub et al. digitise process descriptions into flowsheets and build converged Aspen Plus simulations benchmarked against flowsheets from 50 experts; Tian et al. go from textual specifications to executable process simulations with a multi-agent workflow; Zhang et al. combine a knowledge graph with agents to assemble and calibrate process models for digital twins. The author's distinction: these works stop at flowsheets, simulations and process models and do not manage and verify revisions across the further disciplines of a plant project. The author also cites his own earlier review proposing teams of specialised agents (Rupprecht et al., 2026) and argues for the single general-purpose agent instead, while calling the comparison an open question needing a controlled study.

**Engineering-as-Code and reproducibility practice.** Software engineering's version control, CI/CD, Infrastructure-as-Code, and Engineering-as-Code transfer it to engineering design; Davy Demeyer's Design-Ops work applied it in plant and automation engineering with early industrial pilots pointing to substantially faster design iterations, though those pilots use closed software and their details are confidential and cannot be examined or reproduced. On reproducibility the paper cites rules for reproducible research, the FAIR principles, containers, reproducible builds requiring byte-identical output from the same inputs, and W3C PROV-O for recording which activity and agent produced a result.

## Implications

The following is analysis by the KB maintainers, not a claim of the authors.

The most transferable idea is the **fingerprint-and-provenance discipline**, and it is the clearest instance in this knowledge base of [[concepts/structural-constraint-over-instruction]]: the constraint is not in the prompt, it is in the file format, the schema, the check program and the git history. Three properties are worth copying directly. First, **authoritative versus derived files with input fingerprints**, so that staleness is detected rather than asserted — the mechanism that lets a tool say "this number's inputs changed" instead of a human remembering. Second, **derived files are never hand-edited**, so every value in the repository has one provenance class. Third, **a calculated value never overwrites a given or assumed one**: the tool reports the imbalance rather than silently fixing the input. That is the difference between a checker and a repairer, and it is the property that keeps a human in the loop.

The second takeaway is the **finding-versus-coverage asymmetry**, which the paper demonstrates with unusual honesty: the checks fired on every fault written with knowledge of the rules and named only 4 of 12 faults written blind. The lesson for teams building such layers is that rule *coverage*, not rule correctness, is the scaling bottleneck, and that coverage can only be measured by planting faults whose author has not seen the rules. The paper's own suggestion — benchmarks with planted faults on open plants, so that coverage becomes a measurable quantity — is the most useful methodological export here, and it fits [[techniques/verification-and-evidence]] and the [[concepts/evaluator-circularity]] concern that a checker written by the same team as the designer inherits the designer's blind spots.

Third, the **"0 errors is not correctness"** result is a concrete instance of [[concepts/verified-completion-vs-apparent-success]] and of [[concepts/protocol-relative-scores]]: the check suite's verdict is a property of the rule set, not of the design, and the capacity-change experiment shows a clean check run on an unresized plant. Any organisation wiring such gates into a merge policy should also require the *evidence* — which files were touched, which results are now stale, which reviews were invalidated — rather than reading the status line. That is why the platform records calculation maturity and open warnings next to the errors, and it is the same design choice the paper makes in distinguishing proposed, checked and approved revisions.

Fourth, the **cost shape** is a useful planning input: a complete preliminary basic-engineering package cost USD 224 of API time in 56 sessions over about 27 hours, with layout iteration consuming 40% of that. Drafting is cheap; review was near half the cost once close-out jobs are counted. That supports the author's expectation that the cost of first drafts falls faster than the cost of checking, which sets the pace — meaning the binding constraint on agentic engineering is the human review and certification path, not the tokens. Organisations should therefore invest in review instrumentation and approval records first, and in model throughput second. [[projects/tracybot]] and [[projects/auto-research]] are related entries in this knowledge base on agent-generated work and its verification; these are analyst-level comparisons, not claims of citation or dependency.

Fifth, the **openness bet**: a plant as plain, linked, machine-readable files in git is what makes the reuse the paper envisions possible at all — designs, models, decisions and evidence reusable across organisations, life cycle inventories recalculable from their models, and the Process Atlas as a forthcoming generated database. Note that the paper itself states the Process Atlas's breadth and data quality are not results of this paper.

The scoping is the point of the whole design and is worth restating for adoption: the platform constrains **what can be checked and what is traceable**, not what is correct. That is [[concepts/scoped-assurance]] applied to engineering artefacts — the assurance claim is bounded by the rule set, and a [[concepts/preservation-aware-agent-improvement]] reading follows directly, since a revision that invalidates downstream results must be visible rather than silently applied. This page also contributes to [[evaluations/briefing-synthesis-2026-11-03-to-09]]. These links are analyst-level comparisons, not claims that the projects cite or depend on one another.

## Reproducible experiment

The platform is genuinely open: the core is public under GNU AGPL-3.0-or-later at https://github.com/ChemE-as-Code/core, the project template at https://github.com/ChemE-as-Code/project-template, the process library including the ammonia case at tag `nh3-run-final` at https://github.com/ChemE-as-Code/process-library, the project site at https://cheme-as-code.org, and the section 5.5 evaluation scripts and raw results archived on Zenodo (reference 92 of the paper). This makes replication feasible in a way most pages in this knowledge base cannot offer.

1. **Reproduce the check-coverage curve, which is the paper's central experimental claim.** Take a fresh copy of the ammonia case at `nh3-run-final` and run the checks unmodified, then plant faults in three strata exactly as the paper does: (a) faults written after reading the rules, (b) faults written by a separate agent session that cannot see the checks, skills or reviews, and (c) implausible but self-consistent values. Pass criterion: 19/19 rule-aware faults fire, roughly 4/12 blind faults are *named* (not merely signalled as changed), and 0/4 self-consistent implausibilities are identified. The pass criterion is directional for stratum (b) — if your rule set names most blind faults, coverage has improved beyond the paper's baseline; if it names none, the checks are reporting only change signals, which is the failure mode the paper documents.
2. **Run the capacity-change experiment and check the two claims the paper makes about it.** Have an agent raise capacity to 1,800 t/d with no gate relaxed, then measure: how many of the expected-impact files change, how many lines exceed design velocity by the agent's own screening, and whether any P&ID or equipment dimension changed. Pass criterion: the paper's pattern — 128 of 161 files changed, 0 errors and 252 warnings on rerun, and yet the plant is not resized with 36 lines above design velocity. A run that reports 0 errors *and* a fully resized plant would be a stronger result than the paper's; a run that reports 0 errors and an unresized plant would confirm the central caution.
3. **Check the fingerprint mechanism directly.** Make one upstream edit — for example a feed-temperature change — and rerun. Pass criterion: the out-of-date findings appear with file and line, a simulation rerun of about 72 s clears all 106 of them, and no value is silently overwritten. Also test the regeneration property: rerun the six generators and confirm byte-identical output, and check whether the LCA and MTO line-ending defect still reproduces on a second operating system.
4. **Add the missing comparison the author names.** Run the same brief with the skills removed (agent plus tools only) and with the same agent in a discipline-specific multi-agent arrangement, on the same budget and stop rules. This is the comparison the paper explicitly declines to make, and it is the one that would quantify what the skills contribute.
5. **Do not substitute engineering judgement with the checks.** Any evaluation should be reported with the number of independent-engineer findings per check, because the paper's own evidence shows that agent review and rule coverage both leave real defects (no tube-rupture case, wrong heat-transfer coefficients, missing relief capacity, an unrated HAZOP entry).

Model substitution is unavoidable: the paper used Claude Opus 5.5 and no independent engineer, so any replication inherits both gaps and must report them.
