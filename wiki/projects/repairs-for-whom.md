# Repairs for Whom?

## What it is

"Repairs for Whom? Evaluator–Executor Mismatch in Selecting Repairs for Failed LLM Agents" (Yijun Liao, Independent Researcher; Fanwei Liang, South China University of Technology; arXiv:2610.38610 [cs.SE], v1 submitted 28 Oct 2026) studies what happens when a failed LLM-agent trajectory is repaired automatically. A repair method must decide which step to change and what to put there. A single-step repair r = (t, a′) replaces action a_t at history h_t with alternative a′, after which some **executor** produces the rest of the episode. This paper fixes the failed trajectory and the candidate set, and isolates the second decision: *which evaluator should score and select among the candidates?*

The paper's central concept is that an executor is more than a model name. It comprises the model weights, the prompt and history protocol, the decoding parameters, and the rules for handling invalid actions. The **deployed executor** e₀ is the policy that produced the failure and will run whichever repair is chosen. Candidate evaluators depart from e₀ in two ways: changing the *target* (greedy-decoding success, task solvability, another model's success probability, a judge's opinion) or keeping the target but cutting the *budget* (few rollouts of e₀, or a threshold rule applied to e₀'s own rollouts). Its conclusions are prescriptions about identity, not capability: repairs should be verified with the executor that will run them, and repair labels should name their executor. The authors release code, trajectories, all rollout outcomes, and their analysis plan at https://github.com/lyj20071013/repairs-for-whom.

## Mechanism

For executor e, p_e(h_t, a) is the probability that the episode succeeds within the remaining step budget when action a is taken at h_t and e executes the rest (in WebShop, the expected reward in [0,1]). The **net gain** of a repair under e is R_e(r) = p_e(h_t, a′) − p_e(h_t, a_t). Net gain, not post-repair success, is the primary objective, because the original action can itself succeed on a rerun; the post-repair objective, checked separately, gives the same conclusions. **Matched selection** picks the candidate with the highest estimated R_{e₀} from the deployed executor's own rollouts. The **selection loss** of an evaluator c is L_c = E_τ[ R_{e₀}(r̂⋆) − R_{e₀}(r̂_c) ], where both deployment values are estimated from rollouts neither selection saw (cross-fitting), which keeps the comparison free of the winner's curse; L_c can be negative because matched selection is itself finite-sample. A positive control requires matched selection to beat random by more than δ = 0.10 per pool; all pools passed.

The three environments are text worlds restorable exactly to any intermediate history by replaying the action prefix. A fully observed 5×5 **gridworld** (key, locked door, gem, 30 steps; traps end episodes and create an irreversible point) provides exact solvability and step slack. **ALFWorld** (50-step budget, partially observed household tasks, no irreversible actions) uses a Fast Downward planner for solvability and slack; the two-object task type is excluded because the 7B agent almost never succeeds on it. **WebShop** (original code, 100,000 products, 1,021 human-written instructions, 25-step budget) ends at purchase with reward in [0,1] — the primary objective, fixed before any formal run.

The deployed agents (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) are served with vLLM at temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95, a separate seed per request; each step the agent writes a short thought then one action, earlier thoughts are dropped from the history, and an inadmissible answer is re-asked up to twice without consuming a step. Substitute executors (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct; Qwen3.8-flash via API without reasoning; each deployed model run on the other's failure pool) use exactly the same prompt, history protocol, decoding, and action parsing, so only executor identity varies. The LLM judge is Qwen3.8-flash *with* reasoning: it reads the trajectory and candidate list, is told which model will execute the repair, and names one candidate.

Positions are fixed before candidates are generated. The **unscreened pools** are the core evidence: the first 60 failures of fresh episodes, in collection order, for each deployed agent in ALFWorld and WebShop (60 of 82 and 60 of 109 failures in ALFWorld; 60 of 72 and 60 of 86 in WebShop), with no repair-value screening. Candidates per position combine two proposals from each of three LLMs shown the task, the full failed log, and the admissible actions, one privileged action (the planner's next action in ALFWorld; the next action toward the target product in WebShop), and one random action; WebShop additionally includes every clickable element. Means: 6.0–6.1 candidates per position in ALFWorld, 10.1–11.5 in WebShop. Two **screened pools** (gridworld, 34 trajectories; ALFWorld, 30; kept only where screening showed some repair helps) support the wider evaluator comparison. The unit of analysis is the trajectory; unscreened matched selection is cross-fitted (5 of 10 rollouts select, 5 evaluate, averaged over 50 random splits); cross-executor comparisons use equal rollout counts on both sides; intervals are 90% from 2,000 trajectory-resampling bootstraps; tests are one-sided at α = 0.05, Holm-corrected within pre-specified families of a dated analysis plan.

## Evidence

All figures below are author-reported. Losses are in expected net gain — success probability in ALFWorld and the gridworld, net reward in WebShop — relative to matched selection.

**Primary results (unscreened 60-failure pools; all ten pre-specified primary tests Holm-confirmed).** The cheapest substitute for the deployed agent's judgement — replaying the trajectory greedily, at temperature 0 — already loses 0.08–0.13 of net gain in ALFWorld and 0.06–0.13 in WebShop. At an equal rollout budget (5 rollouts per candidate on each side), selection by the stronger same-family Qwen2.5-14B for the 7B agent loses **0.25 [0.19, 0.32]** in ALFWorld and **0.07 [0.05, 0.10]** in WebShop. Cross-family substitution at similar size loses in both directions in both environments: 0.074 and 0.098 in ALFWorld, 0.081 and 0.166 in WebShop.

| Pool (deployed agent) | Greedy replay | 14B selection, equal budget | Cross-family selection |
|---|---|---|---|
| ALFWorld (Qwen2.5-7B) | 0.135 [0.090, 0.183] | 0.25 [0.19, 0.32] | Llama selecting: 0.074 [0.038, 0.113] |
| ALFWorld (Llama-3.1-8B) | 0.082 [0.048, 0.122] | — | 7B selecting: 0.098 [0.059, 0.141] |
| WebShop (Qwen2.5-7B) | 0.061 [0.036, 0.086] | 0.07 [0.05, 0.10] | Llama selecting: 0.081 [0.046, 0.114] |
| WebShop (Llama-3.1-8B) | 0.131 [0.083, 0.181] | — | 7B selecting: 0.166 [0.117, 0.217] |

The stakes are large: matched selection gains 0.82–0.87 in success probability on the ALFWorld pools and 0.15 (7B pool) and 0.31 (Llama pool) in WebShop reward, so WebShop substitutes forgo 41–55% of the attainable gain. Nor is the pattern an artifact of the privileged candidates: on LLM-proposed candidates only, greedy replay still loses 0.11 in ALFWorld and 14B selection 0.20 (exploratory), and conclusions persist under the post-repair-success objective.

**On the screened pools, the most capable executor lost the most.** At an equal budget of 10 rollouts per candidate, Qwen3.8-flash loses 0.63 [0.55, 0.70] in ALFWorld — worse than the 14B's 0.29 [0.19, 0.40] — and 0.16 in the gridworld, against the 14B's 0.06. On these pools capability is inversely ordered with selection quality. (In WebShop the 14B loses no more than Llama.)

**Why substitutes lose.** Three mechanisms are measured. First, *genuine preference reversals*: on unscreened ALFWorld trajectories where the 7B and 14B argmax candidates differed, 30 fresh rollouts per branch per executor gave Δ_7B = 0.33 [0.23, 0.44] and Δ_14B = −0.18 [−0.25, −0.11] for the value difference between the two candidates — each model still prefers its own choice, with fresh rollouts retaining about 78% of the difference seen in the selecting rollouts. Saturation does not explain this away: in the pre-specified stratum where the 14B succeeds less than half the time keeping the original action, the equal-budget loss is still 0.17 [0.11, 0.24]. Second, *failure to discriminate*: in the 7B–Llama reversal check the 7B preference replicates (Δ_7B = 0.18 [0.10, 0.27]) but Llama's does not (Δ_Llama = −0.02 [−0.07, 0.03]) — Llama's loss comes mainly from failing to separate the repairs the 7B agent needs, a different failure mode from the 14B's stable opposite preferences. Third, *signal type*: environment signals identify what to change, not where — in the gridworld, choosing at random among the actions that restore solvability at the irreversible point loses 0.31 [0.22, 0.40], and in ALFWorld the planner's action is the best candidate in 29 of 30 screened trajectories while the best position's net gain differs by 0.51 on average across a trajectory's three positions. Published *decision rules*, re-implemented as verification steps, lose because they accept rather than rank: on the screened pools, 2-of-3 acceptance loses 0.23–0.32 and single-replay flip 0.28–0.43 across the two pools, earliest-single-success loses 0.58 in the gridworld, and latest-significant-step is nearly lossless there (−0.01) but loses 0.19 in ALFWorld. Log-reading judges fare no better where outcomes depend on unobserved state or post-intervention behaviour: the reasoning judge loses 0.06 (gridworld) but 0.53 (ALFWorld), the deployed model judging its own log 0.59 and 0.74, and a single human annotator gained 0.335 against matched selection's 0.822 (exploratory).

**Rollout exchange rates.** More substitute rollouts buy little. On the unscreened 7B ALFWorld pool, the value of 14B selections rises from 0.38 (1 rollout per candidate) to 0.63 [0.56, 0.71] at 20 rollouts — about the value of a *single* deployed-agent rollout per candidate (0.63), and still below greedy replay (0.69) and five own rollouts (0.82; the own-rollout curve runs 0.63, 0.77, 0.80, 0.82 for k = 1, 2, 3, 5). Twenty 14B rollouts per candidate cost 31–39× the GPU time of the one-own-rollout selection and 6–8× the five-own-rollout selection. In WebShop, twenty 14B rollouts select repairs worth 0.091 reward and ten Llama rollouts 0.080 — between one (0.072) and two (0.106) of the 7B agent's own rollouts. Conversely, **about three of the deployed agent's own rollouts per candidate recover most of matched selection's value**: 3 rollouts lose only 0.11 (gridworld) and 0.07 (ALFWorld) on the screened pools relative to full matched selection, and 0.02 on the unscreened 7B ALFWorld pool relative to five; 10 own rollouts are within noise of the reference on the screened pools.

**Labels and rankings are executor-relative.** Defining a repair-location label as the position of the highest-net-gain candidate, the argmax position computed from 14B rollouts agrees with the deployed agent's in only **32% [26, 38]** of ALFWorld trajectories (Qwen3.8-flash: 29%), while two disjoint halves of the deployed agent's own rollouts agree with each other 62% of the time. Scoring 17 selection methods under different executors' rollouts, the ALFWorld method rankings under 14B and flash scoring correlate only moderately with the ranking under deployed-agent scoring (Kendall τ ≈ 0.51 and 0.46; gridworld 0.77 and 0.60), and each substitute executor ranks *first* the method that uses its own rollouts — methods that rank 12th and 14th under deployed-agent scoring.

**A hybrid works only where the privileged signal is strong.** Keeping only the candidate a privileged signal prefers at each position, then ranking survivors with deployed-agent rollouts, comes within 0.009 [0.004, 0.014] of full matched selection on screened ALFWorld at 19% of its rollout budget, because the planner's action is the best candidate in 86% of unscreened 7B trajectories. In WebShop the privileged action is best in only 29% (7B pool) and 20% (Llama pool) of trajectories, and there the same architecture is no better than a flash-proposal screen on the 7B pool and loses 0.26 on the Llama pool — more than greedy replay. Without any privileged signal, candidate generation is the ceiling: an LLM-locates-and-proposes pipeline gains 0.32 with verification against 0.22 unverified, but its better proposal is worth only 0.37 even in hindsight, against 0.64 for matched selection over the richer fixed candidate sets.

## Limitations

Stated by the authors: only two deployed agents, both open models of 7–8B parameters; three text environments, each restorable exactly to any history; single-step repairs within fixed candidate sets only; the screened pools condition on failures in which some single-step repair helps; and the unscreened pools still restrict task types and repair positions (ALFWorld two-object tasks excluded; positions require at least five steps of slack or room to recover). The label/ranking analyses and the human study are exploratory.

Evident from the methods: the analysis plan was dated and pre-specified, but it **was not independently timestamped or publicly registered** — the pre-registration claim rests on the authors' released, anonymized internal record. The screened pools' interval re-centering (a bias correction for the shrunken-binomial bootstrap) was added after the pre-specified analyses were run; the authors report that decisions are unchanged, but the confirmatory status of those intervals is weaker than the label suggests. The published methods compared (DoVer's 2-of-3 rule, CausalFlow's single-replay flip, AgenTracer's earliest success, Causal Agent Replay's latest significant step) appear **only as re-implemented verification steps** inside this fixed-candidate protocol, not as full reproductions of pipelines that also locate and propose repairs — so the losses attach to the rules in isolation, not to the deployed methods. The primary WebShop objective was switched from success to reward after a 30-instruction pipeline test, before formal runs; disclosed, but a design change made after seeing pilot data. The human study uses a single annotator who is a co-author, and its protocol was not frozen after the pilot as planned. The Llama rollouts for 65 of 1,286 branches of one pool were lost and regenerated with the same seeds and code, so the released data reproduce one headline value slightly differently (0.071 released versus 0.074 recorded) — also disclosed. Only one stronger-model relationship is tested at scale (7B→14B, same family), plus one API model used at 10 rollouts on screened pools and as proposer/judge; no proprietary frontier model serves as an executor on the unscreened pools, and nothing tests weaker, deployment-specialized, or multi-agent executors. Intervals are 90% and tests one-sided, weaker per test than the 95%/two-sided convention. Finally, the protocol itself requires exact restorability of any history and enumerable admissible actions; results may not transfer to tool-use, browser, or OS agents where that is impossible.

## Competing approaches

The paper positions itself against three bodies of work. First, **failure-attribution and repair benchmarks whose labels are executor-defined**: Who&When and Who&When Pro (decisive steps defined by replay), AgenTracer (the earliest step whose correction suffices), the Agent Error Dataset and AgentDebug (root-cause annotation at scale), and localization-only systems such as AgentRx and TrajDebug. These evaluate localization or a single correction; this paper instead labels a fixed candidate set with the deployed policy's outcomes and asks which evaluator *selects* correctly. Second, **counterfactual-replay verification methods** — DoVer, CausalFlow, Causal Agent Replay, minimal-repair families, and Counterfactual Rollout Replay — whose verification steps are re-implemented here as evaluators and lose to matched selection mainly because acceptance rules do not rank candidates; the net-gain objective builds on work showing many apparent repairs succeed by chance. Third, the **process-reward-model backdrop**: Monte-Carlo step values depend on the completing policy, and prior work calibrates or corrects the estimates for that mismatch (smaller completers overestimate; Bayesian corrections; frozen-reference completion with reweighting). This paper's distinction is that it measures what executor dependence *costs in decisions* — selection regret — rather than correcting the estimates.

The closest result in tension is Zhang et al. (2026), who estimate intervention value by prefix branching in ALFWorld with the same two base models and learn oversight controllers (continue / hand to an expert planner / quit) that transfer between the two models with small regret. The tasks differ — a three-way oversight decision, not selection among roughly 6–11 candidate repairs the deployed agent must itself carry out — so it does not refute the selection-loss numbers, but it bounds their generality: coarse intervention decisions may transfer across executors where fine-grained repair selection does not. In spirit, EASE found skill curation tuned for one executor transfers poorly to others, and frozen stronger-model value changes agree in sign with a weaker policy's Monte-Carlo rollouts only 66–72% of the time.

## Implications

For agent engineering — especially routing and selection systems — the results imply (all consequences of the author-reported evidence above):

1. **Verify with the deployment policy, not a bigger sibling.** Escalating *evaluation* to a stronger model while a smaller model *executes* is not an upgrade: at equal budget it cost 0.25 of success probability in ALFWorld, and 20 strong-model rollouts bought no more than 1–2 of the deployed model's own at 6–39× the GPU cost.
2. **"Verified" is executor-scoped metadata.** A repair label, benchmark ground truth, or validation stamp is meaningful only relative to a named executor — weights plus prompt protocol plus decoding plus invalid-action handling. Systems should log which executor validated each repair or label; a model swap, even a same-family size change or a decoding change to greedy, invalidates prior verification. (Temperature 0.3 stayed within the 0.10 margin of the deployed 0.7; greedy did not.)
3. **Cheap verification exists.** Roughly three rollouts of the deployed agent per candidate recover most of matched selection's value when good candidates are present. Verification budget should be spent on the deployed policy's rollouts before any substitute's.
4. **Judges are not executors.** Log-reading judges — including the deployed model judging itself, and a human expert reader — select poorly where outcomes hinge on unobserved state or post-intervention behaviour. A judge may still propose candidates, a weaker requirement, but its ranking should not substitute for rollouts when rollouts are possible.
5. **Privileged signals and rollouts are complements.** Oracles and planners identify *what* to change; deployed rollouts identify *where*. The hybrid screen-then-rank architecture is worth its savings only where the privileged signal is measurably strong (planner action best in 86% of ALFWorld vs 20–29% of WebShop trajectories; there it underperformed greedy replay on one pool). Gate the architecture on measured signal quality.
6. **Benchmark hygiene.** Any benchmark that defines repair or attribution labels by replay should publish the replay executor and, ideally, per-candidate rollout outcomes per executor; rankings produced under a substitute executor correlate only τ ≈ 0.46–0.51 with deployment-relative rankings and systematically crown the method that uses the scoring executor's own rollouts.
7. **Candidate generation is the ceiling in the no-privileged-signal regime.** When an LLM both locates and proposes, verification added +0.10 of gain (0.22 → 0.32), but the candidate ceiling was 0.37 in hindsight against 0.64–0.82 for richer candidate sets — spend on proposer diversity and coverage before verification depth.
8. **Diagnose the substitute's failure mode.** A stronger same-family model may genuinely prefer different repairs (reversals confirmed on fresh rollouts), while a similar-sized cross-family model may simply fail to discriminate among the repairs the deployed agent needs. The remedies differ — use own rollouts in the first case, more of them in the second — so a selection system should distinguish "the evaluator disagrees" from "the evaluator cannot tell."

## Reproducible experiment

The protocol replicates directly from the authors' release — code, trajectories, all rollout outcomes, and the analysis plan (github.com/lyj20071013/repairs-for-whom) — or by re-collecting:

1. Serve Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with vLLM (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95, per-request seeds; thought-then-action prompting; thoughts dropped from history; at most two re-asks on inadmissible actions). Run ALFWorld episodes under the 50-step budget, excluding two-object tasks, and keep the first 60 failures in collection order.
2. For each failure, fix three positions — one each from the early, middle, and late thirds of positions with at least five steps of planner slack (Fast Downward) — and build the candidate set: two proposals each from three LLMs (the deployed model, Qwen2.5-14B, Qwen3.8-flash) shown the task, the full failed log, and the admissible actions; the planner's next action; one random admissible action.
3. Roll out every branch — 10× with the 7B agent, 20× with Qwen2.5-14B, plus one greedy branch each — and compute net gains against the original action's branch.
4. Cross-fit over 50 random 5/5 splits: matched selection takes the argmax net gain on the selection fold; score all selectors (matched, greedy replay, 14B at 5 rollouts, the 3-own-rollout rule, random) on the held-out fold.
5. Pre-specify the two primary contrasts (matched vs greedy; own-5 vs 14B-5), Holm-correct within the family, report selection losses with 90% percentile intervals from 2,000 trajectory-resampling bootstraps, and check the positive control (matched minus random > 0.10).
6. Optionally, run the mechanism check: on trajectories where the 7B and 14B argmaxes differ, take 30 fresh rollouts per branch per executor and test whether Δ_7B > 0 > Δ_14B.

Expected replication targets (author-reported): greedy-replay loss ≈ 0.13, 14B-at-5 loss ≈ 0.25, matched-selection gain ≈ 0.82 on this pool.

## Evidence status

All quantitative results on this page are **author-reported** from a single study; the paper cites no independent replication of its own results, and none is claimed here. The internal robustness checks — pre-specified tests with Holm correction, a positive control per pool, fresh-rollout confirmation of the preference-reversal result, and second-objective and LLM-only-subset re-checks — are part of the same study and do not constitute independent replication. This page replaces the 2026-10-30 briefing import, whose summary and metrics had not been independently reverified; the upgrade is written from a full reading of the arXiv v1 text (Sections 1–9, Appendices A–J); every figure above is as reported there. The one adjacent result in tension (small-regret cross-model transfer of oversight controllers) concerns a different task and is recorded under Competing approaches.
